# Copy Journable's Ease, Then Beat Its Numbers

Journable feels fast because it removes steps. Its AI is not what makes it quick. One chat bar takes a typed sentence or a photo. There is no signup questionnaire, no meal-slot picker and no database search, and the AI's itemised guess goes straight into the diary, where you edit it afterwards. Its accuracy is something users feel, not something anyone has shown. No journalist, dietitian or lab has tested it against weighed food. Its website says "no databases needed" and its privacy policy names OpenAI as its AI provider. The users who check it against labels or a kitchen scale report the same meal changing by 50% from one day to the next, half a large McDonald's fries logged at 75 kcal, and 104 g of protein in a 200 g steak. That pattern fits numbers written by a language model rather than looked up in a database. So Journable's biggest strength (4.7 stars from about 124,000 Play ratings, 1M+ downloads and a real Indian user base) sits on top of its biggest weakness: you cannot trust its numbers to come out the same twice. Its most common one-star complaint is something else again: a paywall after roughly 20 lifetime entries that users say nobody told them about. For STEADY the plan splits cleanly. Copy Journable's low effort, then win on everything that needs a database and a memory: identical numbers for identical meals, corrections that teach the app, barcodes and label reading, health sync, and an honest free tier. The uncomfortable part is that STEADY's own pipeline lets guesswork in the same way, just in different places. The model guesses grams. A search with no sort order can pick a different row for the same food. Uncapped AI estimates and a few bad INDB rows push numbers up. A shared cache froze mistakes for every user. The fix is to shrink the model's job to choosing a food ID and a unit from fixed lists. Ranked search, checked data, portion tables and each user's own history then produce every number, and an evaluation that measures signed bias (whether errors lean high or low) and repeat-run consistency judges the result. ±10% is reachable for packaged food, food weighed on a scale, and repeat meals whose portions the app has learned. For a first-time photo of a mixed Indian plate, the honest target is "consistent and not skewed high or low".

## Journable is fast because it deletes steps, not because its AI is clever

**Journable: AI Calorie Counter** is a chat-first tracker from Journable LLC, a small company with a Seattle private-mailbox address. Its privacy policy is issued by a related company, Multicoded LLC ([Privacy policy](https://app.termly.io/document/privacy-policy/b256e222-60d2-42e0-b7ea-379548211541)). It launched on iOS on 8 March 2024 and on Android five days later. The co-founders are Steve Hoyek and a partner named only as Georges ([Product Hunt makers](https://www.producthunt.com/products/journable/makers)). Most of its users are on Android: **4.7 stars from about 124,000 Play ratings and 1M+ downloads**, against 518 ratings on the US App Store ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&hl=en_US); [iTunes Lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6477719720&country=us)). India matters to it. **India is its second-largest iOS storefront by rating count, with 443 ratings against 518 in the US** ([iTunes Lookup IN](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6477719720&country=in)). The Android app is built in Flutter, with Firebase analytics, Adjust install tracking, RevenueCat billing and AdMob and Facebook ads ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/com.journable.app)). All twenty-five iOS releases in 2026 carry the same note, "Improvements to the AI chat for simpler calorie tracking," so the changelog says nothing about work on speed or accuracy ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720)).

Every log starts in one bar fixed to the bottom of the home screen. It is labelled "What did you eat or exercise?" and has three icons beside it: a bookmark for favourites and recent entries, a gallery picker and a camera ([App Store screenshots](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720)). Typing "Grilled Salmon Fillet with Steamed Broccoli and a Cup of Brown Rice" produces a meal card with three lines, **each showing its assumed portion in its name**: "Grilled Salmon (3 oz)", "Steamed Broccoli (1 cup)" and "Brown Rice (1 cup)". Each line has its own calories and macros. The card adds a meal total, a percentage of the daily target, a timestamp and an edit pencil. There is no breakfast/lunch/dinner picker anywhere, and cards are simply timestamped. Exercise goes into the same box ("45 minutes tennis" becomes 400 kcal), so a model is clearly deciding what each message is without the user picking a mode. An independent QA firm's test script shows the text flow is literally: tap the field, type "Cappuccino", tap Return ([QAwerk](https://bugcrawl.qawerk.com/weekly-bug-crawl/bugs-found-in-journable-ai-calorie-counter-for-ios/)). Working from the screens, not from measurement, that is three actions for typed food, two taps for a repeat favourite and two taps for a photo. It is unclear whether a review screen comes before saving. Journable's July 2026 blog says users "review and adjust the result before saving" ([Journable blog](https://www.journable.com/blog/how-accurate-are-ai-calorie-counters)), but the QA steps and screenshots look like log first, edit after.

What is missing matters as much as what is there. **There is no barcode scanner.** A Play user asked for one in October 2026, and the developer treated it as a feature suggestion ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&hl=en_US)). **There is no official voice input.** Only an unaffiliated fan site claims it ([journable.net](https://journable.net/)), and a site that summarises reviews lists input as text or photo only ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/journable-ai-calorie-counter/6477719720)). **There is no Apple Health or Health Connect sync.** The developer called it "on the roadmap and coming soon" in March 2025, and users were still asking for it in October 2026. No Apple Watch app or widgets turned up. Memory is limited to favourites, recents, saved meals and auto-complete of past entries. Even these are hard to find: users who asked for "regular meals" and named meals were told by the developer that the feature already exists ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720?see-all=reviews)).

All of the speed evidence comes from what users say. Reviewers call it "as easy as writing a text message" ([App Store DE](https://itunes.apple.com/de/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6477719720/sortBy=mostRecent/json)) and "just one click away after opening the app" ([Play review](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=9da0fa74-ca54-48cd-a248-a19365f24916)). They also praise being able to start "as soon as you open the app, no 21 questions or login hassle" ([Play review](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=c5649f66-c0c5-4c66-94dc-222b5557ecf5)). **No independent timing or tap-count study includes Journable.** The only speed figures for the category are 3–15 seconds for photo logging against 45–65 seconds or more for database search. They come from competitors' blogs and were read only as search-result snippets ([Nutrola](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/best-app-to-track-calories-fast)). Reliability problems also slow it down: users report the app stuck on "analysis" with no result ([Play review](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=6becf8be-2d11-4342-b56d-6f92a859a29d)).

The explanation that fits all of this is that Journable's speed comes from asking fewer questions per log: no search results to scan, no serving-size dialog, no meal slot. It does not come from a faster model. That is good news for STEADY, because removing decisions is a product choice, not a research problem. One Journable habit actually costs time. Each entry gets an AI-written prose "Explanation" ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720)). OpenAI's own latency guide says output length drives response time, and that "cutting 50% of your output tokens may cut ~50% of your latency" ([OpenAI latency guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)). A small source badge tells the user more and costs nothing to generate.

On the AI side, Journable discloses one fact. Its AI features come from third-party providers "including OpenAI", covering "Image analysis, Natural language processing, AI automation" ([Privacy policy](https://app.termly.io/document/privacy-policy/b256e222-60d2-42e0-b7ea-379548211541)). No model is named. No OpenAI code library appears inside the app, which fits AI calls going through Journable's own servers ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/com.journable.app)). Its marketing says plainly that there is no lookup step: "Log meals and exercise with a user-friendly AI chat, **no databases needed**" ([journable.com](https://www.journable.com)). No screenshot shows where a number came from. The most likely design is a single call to a model that reads both text and images and returns item names, portions, calories and macros directly. That is an inference; Journable has not confirmed it. Edits also seem to go back through the model instead of being scaled by simple arithmetic. A user who halved a 400 g, 700 kcal fish package got 250 or 450 kcal instead of 350 ([mwm.ai](https://mwm.ai/apps/journable-ai-calorie-counter/6477719720)). QAwerk saw a 620 kcal meal drop to 356 kcal after deleting a 0.001 tbsp olive-oil line ([QAwerk](https://bugcrawl.qawerk.com/weekly-bug-crawl/bugs-found-in-journable-ai-calorie-counter-for-ios/)).

Revenue comes from a hard paywall. Users get a free allowance of AI entries, usually reported as about 20 and sometimes as 12 or fewer. After that it costs **US$9.99 a month or $39.99 a year** on iOS, and the free tier shows ads ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720); [iTunes Lookup](https://itunes.apple.com/lookup?id=6477719720&country=us)). Prices drop sharply in some countries; one review site quotes an annual price of "a little under $13.00" ([AppsPirate](https://appspirate.com/?p=10956)). The developer says the subscription exists "to help cover the costs of AI technology" ([Google Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&hl=en_US)). An affiliate scheme pays 50% of a referred subscriber's revenue for up to a year ([Affiliate program](https://www.journable.com/affiliate-program)). Third-party estimates put it at about 119,000 monthly active users and "$10K+" a month in revenue. These are unverified ([AppGoblin](https://appgoblin.info/apps/com.journable.app)), but if they are roughly right they point to a large audience that pays little and is mostly on Android.

## Users praise the ease and catch the errors, and nobody has weighed Journable's food

The headline ratings measure how comfortable the app feels, not how correct it is. In the 200 newest Play reviews (September to November 2026), **70% are five stars and 8.5% one star**. Of the 26 one- and two-star reviews among them, 13 are about payment and only 3 about accuracy. Across all 250 low-star Play reviews collected since July 2025, **56% mention the paywall, subscription or trial, and 16% mention accuracy or inconsistency** ([Google Play reviews](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app)). The positive comments on accuracy are vague ("the AI somehow always gets it right") or openly unsure ("not too sure about the accuracy but at least it gives a rough estimates"). The negative ones nearly always cite a label, a restaurant figure or a scale.

Store ratings don't separate methods either. Journable (4.70), SnapCalorie (4.69) and MacroFactor (4.76) sit within a tenth of a star of each other on Play. Yet they produce their numbers in very different ways: a language model, photo depth estimation and a checked database ([Journable Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app); [SnapCalorie Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.snapcalorie.alpha002); [MacroFactor Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.sbs.diet)). So accuracy problems are invisible to casual users and decisive for engaged ones. STEADY's lean-gainer persona belongs to the engaged group.

The specific failures fall into five groups, and each one shows a different gap in an approach where the model writes the numbers.

These symptoms are what you would expect when a language model writes the numbers, and the reason matters for STEADY's own design. Even with the model's randomness setting turned down to zero, AI servers do not give identical outputs. Thinking Machines Lab sent one prompt 1,000 times at "temperature 0" (the setting meant to remove randomness) and got **80 different completions**. The cause is that results depend on how many other users' requests the server happens to be processing at the same moment ([Thinking Machines](https://thinkingmachines.ai/blog/defeating-nondeterminism-in-llm-inference/)). OpenAI's `seed` parameter, a setting meant to make outputs repeatable, only makes them "mostly" identical ([OpenAI cookbook archive](https://git.blob42.xyz/Archives/openai-cookbook/blame/commit/90c6ce1e545c416af681010220493272e0f0f462/examples/Deterministic_outputs_with_the_seed_parameter.ipynb)). Anthropic's API version of 21 October 2026 rejects the `temperature` setting entirely ([Anthropic migration guide](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/api/migration-2026-10-21)). Nutrition studies show the same drift. When researchers asked ChatGPT about 222 foods five times each, the energy answers stayed steady (varying by less than 10%) for only about 35–48% of them ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/health-technology/chatgpt-provides-accurate-nutritional-information)). An app that asks the model for calories inherits all of this. An app that asks the model only *which* food was eaten, and looks up the calories, does not. A German reviewer named the difference exactly: the nutrition values "are often wrong, even though the product was correctly recognized" ([App Store DE](https://itunes.apple.com/de/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6477719720/sortBy=mostRecent/json)). Recognising the food is not Journable's bottleneck; the numbers are.

No weighed-food benchmark includes Journable. The closest test of similar apps points the same way. NIH researchers photographed 102 meals from a metabolic kitchen, where every ingredient is weighed. Cal AI, MyFitnessPal, Lose It! and Appediet **underestimated energy by 252–345 kcal per meal, about a third**, and missed roughly 30 g of fat. This is a July 2026 conference abstract that has not yet been peer-reviewed ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260804/ai-photobased-calorietracking-tools-underestimate-them-by-33); [HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/nutrition/how-accurate-are-photo-based-calorie-apps-4-are-put-to-the-test)). Journable users' checked complaints about meat, fries and Huel mostly fit that under-count on high-calorie foods, while the protein complaints run the other way. A 50-meal test reporting 8.6% error for MacroFactor and 11–13% for MyFitnessPal and Lose It! left Journable out, and was published by a competitor that ranked itself first ([Nutrola](https://nutrola.app/en/blog/every-ai-calorie-tracking-app-ranked-2026-independent-accuracy-test)). Journable itself makes no measurable claim. Its own accuracy article says the figures it describes "should not be treated as a performance claim for Journable" ([Journable blog](https://www.journable.com/blog/how-accurate-are-ai-calorie-counters)).

Evidence on Indian food is thin and split. Only 15 of 593 Play reviews mention Indian dishes, regional foods or types of rice. One calls the app "Not very Indian cuisine friendly. And miscalculates portions" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=8bcb7f0a-e7c4-4007-b4b4-aa0a62920a43)). Another says it can "perfectly analyse indian food" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=9c45b3a4-d0c2-4f4b-b6ef-ff9f13c2664d)). Indian iOS reviewers mostly praise its price compared with MyFitnessPal and HealthifyMe. One notes it "is unable to detect calories based of the food brand I mention" ([App Store IN](https://itunes.apple.com/in/rss/customerreviews/page=1/id=6477719720/sortBy=mostHelpful/json)). The app ships in English plus 71 other languages, including Hindi ([App Store](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720)). Nothing shows how it handles Hinglish, a katori or a count of rotis.

## Copy the empty box: the low-effort habits worth taking from Journable

Journable's best ideas are cheap to copy. They should come first, because its reviews show that ease is what earns the stars.

**Let people log before they set anything up.** "No 21 questions or login hassle" is a recurring compliment ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=c5649f66-c0c5-4c66-94dc-222b5557ecf5)). For STEADY, that would mean letting the first log happen before sign-in and goal setup, and asking for goals after the user has seen a meal logged. This is a product decision for the founder, not a proven result.

**Keep one box for everything.** Journable's single bar, which takes food and exercise with no mode switch, backs up STEADY's decision to send speech, typing and photos into one parser.

**Drop meal slots.** Timestamped cards take one decision out of every log. Slots are guesses anyway: real meal times drift by about 1.5–2 hours from day to day ([Tran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12340925/)).

**Put the assumption in the line item.** "Brown Rice (1 cup)" lets a user spot a wrong portion at a glance. STEADY's version is "Dal tadka · 1 katori · 150 g", with portion chips (small tap buttons such as S, M, L) next to it.

**Keep repeats one tap from the box, and make sure people can find them.** In a dataset of 2.66 million logs, **half of users had just nine foods making up half of everything they logged** ([Tran et al. 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12340925/)). Yet Journable users asked for saved meals that already existed. STEADY's "your usual" cards should therefore appear on their own rather than hide behind a bookmark icon.

**Log first, edit after.** Nielsen Norman Group, a UX research firm, advises showing a confirmation dialog only when it prevents errors, and offering undo instead for frequent actions that are easy to reverse ([NN/g](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/confirmation-dialog/)). That fits the founder's rule of auto-logging when the app is confident.

Four Journable habits STEADY should deliberately avoid:

**Prose explanations.** They slow every response and the user cannot check them. A source badge ("IFCT", "Your usual", "AI estimate") is faster and more honest.

**Corrections that pile up.** Corrections typed into a chat thread create duplicate entries. STEADY's corrections should change the original item in place.

**Edits that change untouched numbers.** When an edit is sent back to the model, numbers the user never touched can move. In STEADY, changing the grams should rescale only that line, by plain arithmetic (grams × calories per 100 g), which the existing design already does.

**Photos with no words.** A photo you cannot add words to ("you can't add any text with a photo" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=35a44b47-abad-4664-bcb7-2a6577c1af50))) throws away the user's best information. STEADY's photo log should accept a spoken or typed caption, such as "two rotis, dal, ghee on top".

## Seven openings Journable has left, and how STEADY takes each one

**Numbers that come from a database, with the source shown.** Journable users ask for exactly what it lacks: "it needs a calorie database to search if it gets what I photographed wrong" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=f25f5aed-38db-41c6-8f5b-d96549ac9bb6)). Technical users say the same about AI trackers in general: "I wouldn't trust a pure AI solution without some proper database behind it" ([HN](https://news.ycombinator.com/item?id=44224152)). STEADY's founding rule is the right base: numbers come from the cache, USDA, INDB or IFCT, and an AI estimate is only a flagged last resort. The redesign should show the source on every line and offer a search box when the match is wrong.

**The same meal gives the same number, every time.** This is the cheapest promise to make, and the one Journable's design cannot keep. STEADY should answer "same as yesterday" and "the same Pepper Jack as yesterday" from the user's own history before calling any model. An identical sentence should return identical items, grams and calories. That also makes protein reliable for gainers, because protein then comes from a fixed database row rather than a fresh guess.

**Corrections that edit in place and teach the app.** In a three-week field study, **users edited 20.7% of AI logs and deleted 5.8%** ([SnappyMeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03907v1)). Journable throws that signal away. STEADY should store every confirmed portion and food choice for each user and reuse it (fix 6 below).

**Barcodes and labels read as data, not guessed.** Journable has no barcode scanner, and its label photos are a top complaint. In India, Open Food Facts lists only **about 10,000 products against roughly 90,000 on sale** ([OFF blog](https://blog.openfoodfacts.org/en/news/open-food-facts-india-database-reaches-10k-product-milestone)), so a label-photo reader matters more there than a barcode lookup. Reading a label is copying, not estimating. The model copies the printed per-100 g or per-serving values. Code checks them against the calories their protein, carbs and fat imply (the Atwater check, explained in fix 3) and against the stated serving size. The user confirms once. The result is saved as that user's or that brand's own row, and never overwrites a shared generic food. Barcode scanning should stay free; when MyFitnessPal put it behind a paywall in 2022, users publicly left ([Digital Trends](https://digitaltrends.com/phones/myfitnesspal-barcode-scanning-not-free-premium-subscription)). A related trick is photographing the kitchen-scale display, which one Journable user specifically praised ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=50d39b6d-8239-4871-900a-8e13343887bc)). It is a natural way in to STEADY's optional scale mode.

**Health sync and goals that adapt.** Journable has said Apple Health integration was "coming soon" since March 2025 ([App Store reviews](https://apps.apple.com/us/app/journable-ai-calorie-counter/id6477719720?see-all=reviews)). Its goals don't adjust after weigh-ins, and changing them "will mess up all your previous entries" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=6096b41f-b780-43df-a843-b0d8beee6cc5)). STEADY would need Health Connect on Android, where most Indian users are, and HealthKit on iOS. Both need native code that Expo Go cannot load. STEADY runs on Expo Go today, so this means moving to a "development build" (a custom version of the app that can include extra native code), the same move the widget plan already requires. That last point is an inference from STEADY's own setup.

**An honest free tier that users can see.** Anger at the paywall drives most of Journable's one-star reviews. One user suggested a daily limit "instead of having total limited entries" ([Play](https://play.google.com/store/apps/details?id=com.journable.app&reviewId=991a24a2-0688-4d99-a0d5-890b3cf46a16)). STEADY has no monetisation in v1, so this is a free positioning win now and a design rule for later. Show a usage meter from day one, prefer a daily allowance to a lifetime one, and never put barcodes behind a paywall. The cost table in fix 11 shows that parsing a log costs **$0.04–0.54 per user per month** on current small models, so a generous free tier fits inside the $1–3 AI budget.

**Voice, with the Hinglish risk handled.** Journable has no official voice input, so STEADY's one-tap voice is a real advantage, but only if speech recognition copes with Indian speech. Word error rate is the share of words a speech-to-text system gets wrong. On an April 2026 Indian Hindi benchmark, OpenAI's GPT-4o Transcribe scored **34%** and gpt-4o-mini-transcribe 19.6%, against about 5–6% for Sarvam and Gemini 3 Pro ([Voice of India](https://voiceofindiablogs.ai.joshtalks.com/)). The audio was phone-line quality and the organiser sells speech data, but the gap is large enough that STEADY should test speech services on real food clips before choosing one.

## STEADY's pipeline lets guesswork in at six points

On paper, STEADY's core rule already beats Journable's design. The parse step returns foods and grams. `macroResolver.ts` finds calories per 100 g, and code multiplies, so the model never writes the final calories. The problem is that guesswork gets back in through six side doors, and each one produces a symptom users have reported. The table names the code where each one lives and the fix in the next section that closes it.

| # | Where guesswork enters today | Why it produces the symptom | Symptom | Closed by |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | gpt-4o-mini picks `quantity_g` using the `GRAM_HINTS_PROMPT` table in `macroResolver.ts` (roti 40 g, katori 150 g, single-dish plate 200 g, full main-course plate 350–450 g), and none of these values have been checked | Portion size is the biggest source of error, and models do worse with household units than with grams ([NutriBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)); the model is told to use the table, not forced to | Too high if the defaults are bigger than real home portions; inconsistent when the model reads the same sentence differently | Fixes 5, 6 |
| 2 | Fuzzy search runs `.textSearch('name', norm, {type:'websearch'}).limit(3)` with no sort order (about line 262) | Postgres returns any three matching rows, in whatever order its query planner produces, and that order can change after inserts or routine database maintenance; English word-stemming also mangles Hindi words written in English letters ([Supabase](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/ai/hybrid-search)) | Same food, different rows | Fix 4 |
| 3 | A live USDA API search adds more candidates, then an LLM picks one and can write per-100 g values with no limits | Each item has to wait on outside search results that can change, plus an extra AI call, and nothing checks the invented numbers | Odd or inflated numbers; slow | Fixes 7, 8 |
| 4 | Bad rows in the data: INDB kachori at 713 kcal/100 g; INDB applied cooking-loss adjustments to only 42.62% of recipe ingredients ([INDB paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795/)); raw ingredient rows matched to cooked dishes | One bad row is copied into every log of that food | Too high | Fix 3 |
| 5 | A wrong match was saved to the shared cache under its cleaned-up name and served to every user forever; label photos could overwrite shared rows | One mistake becomes everyone's permanent number | Wrong for everyone, and it changes when rows get overwritten | Fix 2 |
| 6 | A user's correction fixes only that one log | The next log gets the same wrong guess | Users correct the same thing again and lose trust | Fix 6 |

The kachori row shows how cheap some of these fixes are. Pure fat holds 9 kcal per gram, so no food can go above about 900 kcal per 100 g ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/3/Y5022E/y5022e04.htm)). For a kachori to reach 713 kcal per 100 g, even with zero water and everything else pure carbohydrate, at least 63 g of every 100 g would have to be fat. That is not a believable fried pastry, and one line of code can catch it.

The direction of STEADY's error is itself a clue. Every independent test of photo AI finds under-counting: by about a third in the NIH study, and by more as portions grow ([Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260804/ai-photobased-calorietracking-tools-underestimate-them-by-33); [PMC12513282](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12513282/)). STEADY's users report the opposite. That points away from the model's judgement and toward STEADY's own data and defaults. The likely suspects are hint-table portions larger than real home servings (the 350–450 g full-plate rule is a prime candidate), fried-snack outliers like the kachori row, recipe values computed without full cooking adjustments, raw rows matched to cooked dishes, and uncapped AI estimates. This is a hypothesis, not a measurement. Fix 1 exists to find out which of these matters most.

The 65% headline needs the same care. It was last measured on 2 August 2026, and STEADY's own eval notes show three identical runs spreading about two points. Its tolerance is not NutriBench's, which counts a meal as correct when carbs are within ±7.5 g (best published result: 66.8%, GPT-4o reasoning step by step) ([NutriBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)). So the claim that it "matches the frontier" holds only loosely. More importantly, a pass rate says nothing about whether errors lean high or low, or whether results repeat, and those are exactly what users are complaining about.

### Measure direction and repeatability before changing anything (fix 1)

Every later fix needs a ruler, and today's ruler measures only one thing, the pass rate. The upgraded eval should report six things on every run.

**Signed mean error.** This is the average error with its sign kept, so it directly measures "too high".

**Median absolute error and the share within ±10% and ±20%.** Report these per item, per meal and per day, because the ring shows the day total and item errors partly cancel out.

**Breakdowns.** Split results by food category (breads, dals and curries, fried snacks, drinks, sweets, packaged food), by cuisine and by input mode (text, voice, photo).

**Protein error as its own number**, because that is what gainers care about.

**Repeat consistency.** Run every input five times, and once a day for a week to catch changes on the provider's side. Report the share of inputs that come back with identical food IDs and calories. This follows the five-repeat method used in the ChatGPT nutrition study ([HealthDay](https://www.healthday.com/healthpro-news/health-technology/chatgpt-provides-accurate-nutritional-information)).

**Oracle tests.** These work like unit tests with a perfect mock. Give the pipeline the true food, and what's left is portion plus database error. Give it the true grams, and what's left is matching plus database error. Give it both, and what's left is pure database error, which is where outliers like the kachori row show up. Whichever bucket is largest gets fixed first.

Ground truth should be ranked by trustworthiness. Best are weighed home meals computed from IFCT/INDB ingredients. Next are NutriBench's human-checked meal descriptions from 11 countries including India ([NutriBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)), then Nutrition5k cafeteria photos. AI-labelled data should never be used as ground truth. Add the inputs that break parsers: Hinglish, "do roti" ("two rotis"), brand names, and sentences with no quantity. Keep the tuning set separate from the reporting set. STEADY already learned that tuning on dataset-v3 inflated scores by about 12 points over unseen data. Finally, run the same held-out set through Journable. Its free entries, or one month at $9.99, buy the first measured head-to-head comparison anyone has published.

### Stop the shared data being poisoned, then quarantine the outliers (fixes 2 and 3)

Turn off shared-cache writes from any match that wasn't verified. Send label photos to a per-user or per-brand row. Mark the AI-estimated rows already in the cache as suspect. Then run every row, at import and on every AI estimate, through cheap checks that need no AI:

**The Atwater check.** Recompute calories as 4 × protein + 4 × carbs + 9 × fat (plus 7 per gram of alcohol and about 2 per gram of fibre) and flag a row when this differs from its stated calories by more than 15–20% ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/3/Y5022E/y5022e04.htm)). FAO notes that sources legitimately differ by a few percent, so the threshold is this report's proposal.

**Mass balance.** Protein, carbs and fat together cannot exceed about 102 g per 100 g.

**A hard ceiling** of 900 kcal per 100 g.

**Category ranges.** Calculate from the data itself the range that the middle 98% of each category falls in (1st to 99th percentile), and flag anything outside it.

**A cross-source outlier test.** For foods with three or more sources, flag any value far from the median of the others. The measure of "far" is based on the median difference rather than the mean, so one wild value can't drag the yardstick.

**Raw versus cooked.** Every row gets an explicit state: raw, cooked or as-prepared. Cooking changes water content and so calories per 100 g, which is why USDA publishes cooking-yield tables ([USDA cooking yields](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf)).

The international food-matching guidelines add one rule worth adopting outright: don't copy an energy value on its own, but calculate it from the macros ([INFOODS](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/upload/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_final_july2011.pdf)). Flagged rows should be pushed down the rankings rather than deleted, so there is a record of why. Note that the Atwater check catches a row whose macros don't add up to its calories. If the kachori row's macros do add up to 713, it is the category range and cross-source tests that will catch it.

### Make search return the same answer for the same words (fix 4)

Today's search is like looping over a Java `HashSet`: you get the right members in no guaranteed order, and `LIMIT 3` cuts off whatever happens to come first. The fix is to give the database a complete sort order. Here is what that looks like.

**Clean up the words.** Lowercase, remove accents, strip quantities and convert plurals to singular.

**Check an alias table first.** The table maps names for the same food to one entry, with language and script columns (roti = chapati = phulka = रोटी; dal = daal = dhal). It is cheap, predictable and easy to audit.

**If there is no alias hit, run three searches inside one SQL function.** The first is full-text search, ranked by `ts_rank_cd`, using the `'simple'` configuration so Hindi words written in English letters aren't mangled by English word-stemming. The second is trigram search (`pg_trgm`), which compares three-letter chunks, so "panir" matches "paneer" and `word_similarity` finds "paneer" inside "paneer butter masala" ([PostgreSQL docs](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/pgtrgm.html)). The third is embedding search (`pgvector`). An embedding is a numeric fingerprint of meaning, so "anda curry" and "egg curry" land close together.

**Combine the three rankings with Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF).** Each search gives its candidates a score of 1 ÷ (50 + rank), the scores are added up, and a food ranked well by several methods wins. Supabase's reference version uses exactly this, with k = 50 and equal weights ([Supabase hybrid search](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/ai/hybrid-search)).

**End with a complete sort order:** `ORDER BY score DESC, source_priority ASC, food_id ASC`. Ties are always broken the same way, and a curated row beats a USDA or AI-estimate row when scores are close.

**Pass the top ~8 candidates to the model** using strict structured output. The model must return one of those IDs or "none". It never returns a number.

Every commercial food-text service works this way. Nutritionix, Edamam and FatSecret all turn text into ranked candidates from their own database, each with named household measures and gram weights ([Nutritionix](https://docx.syndigo.com/developers/docs/natural-language-for-nutrients); [Edamam](https://developer.edamam.com/food-database-api-docs); [FatSecret NLP](https://platform.fatsecret.com/docs/v1/natural.language.processing)). Embeddings cost almost nothing: about **$0.00006 per user per month** at text-embedding-3-small's $0.02 per million tokens ([OpenAI pricing](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/pricing)). Three caveats apply. RRF's defaults are not tuned for food. How `pg_trgm` behaves on Devanagari script is not documented. And only its GiST index type, not GIN, supports ordering results by similarity. All three need testing on STEADY's eval set.

### Turn grams into data the model never invents (fix 5)

The model should return `{food, count, unit, size}`. The unit comes from a fixed list (piece, katori, bowl, cup, plate, tbsp, tsp, glass, slice, g, ml) and the size from another (small, medium, large or none). Think of these lists like Java enums: the model cannot produce a value outside them. OpenAI's strict structured outputs scored 100% on matching the required format in its own test ([OpenAI](https://openai.com/index/introducing-structured-outputs-in-the-api/)). That guarantees the shape of the answer, not its content, which is why the content must be small categories rather than free numbers.

Code then looks up grams in a `food_portion(food_id, unit, size, grams, source)` table. If there is no entry, it falls back to a default for the category, then to a global default for the unit.

The table's sources exist but don't settle the Indian values. USDA's FNDDS holds **22,046 portion weights** ([FNDDS factsheet](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/FNDDS_2021_2023_factsheet.pdf)). INDB gives serving sizes for its 1,014 recipes, with gaps for pickles ([INDB paper](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795/)). ICMR-NIN's 2024 dietary guidelines contain an annex of household utensil measures that this research could not extract ([ICMR-NIN DGI 2024](https://www.nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI24thJune2024fin.pdf)). The katori sizes found (360, 200, 155 and 115 ml) come from a commercial blog that credits them to NIN ([Instacuppa, secondary](https://instacuppastore.com/blogs/main-page/ladle-size-guide-how-much-dal-one-karchhi-pours-and-why-yours-dribbles)). Two non-authoritative sources put a roti at 30 g and 50 g ([Netmeds](https://www.netmeds.com/health-library/post/what-is-portion-size-what-is-the-difference-between-portion-vs-serving-size-tips-to-measure); [OdishaBytes](https://odishabytes.com/?p=260820)), against STEADY's 40 g. The cheapest authoritative data is STEADY's own: weigh 20–30 common portions (a katori of dal, a roti, a plate of rice) across several households, and keep regional variants (North and South Indian rotis and dosas likely differ, which is unverified).

Ask about portions sparingly. In the SnappyMeal study, follow-up questions made calorie error *worse*, from 148.9 to 161.8 kcal ([SnappyMeal](https://arxiv.org/html/2511.03907v1)). So show portion chips only when the portion is genuinely uncertain *and* the item carries a lot of calories at its default size, for example over 150 kcal. That threshold is this report's proposal.

### Make each user's history the first database (fix 6)

This is the founder's "shared checked base plus per-user learning" decision, turned into data structures.

**A per-user cache.** It works like Python's `functools.lru_cache`, a lookup table keyed by the input. The key is a fingerprint of the parser version, the language and the cleaned-up sentence. The stored value is the resolved list of food ID, unit, size and grams, and deliberately not the calories. Calories are recalculated from the current database every time, so a fix to the data reaches every past and future log.

**Learned portions.** For each user, food and unit, use the median of the last ~5 confirmed gram values once at least two exist. A median ignores one-off typos.

**History first.** "Same as yesterday", "mera usual nashta" ("my usual breakfast") and repeat favourites are answered from history before any search or model call.

**Sharing corrections.** A user's correction updates their own numbers straight away. It reaches other users only after several of them independently agree, or after a check against a reference source passes. No study of portion learning in calorie apps was found, so this design is engineering judgement. What is well supported is how much people repeat foods, which is what makes per-user memory pay off.

### Keep the AI fallback on a short leash and bring USDA in-house (fixes 7 and 8)

When nothing matches, the model should return protein, carbs and fat per 100 g, never calories. Code then calculates calories with the Atwater formula, clamps the result to the food category's range, and saves it once as an `ai_estimate` row with the lowest priority, queued for human review. The same unknown food then gets the same number forever, and it gets better when a person fixes the row.

Separately, bulk-import USDA's Foundation, SR Legacy and FNDDS data (public domain) into Postgres, and take the live API off the logging path. That removes a network round trip on each new food and an outside ranking that can change. It also ends the class of bug in which a wrongly built USDA request silently disabled the prepared-dish data for months. Keep USDA's Branded data last, because its product names are noisy.

### Spend the 2.5 seconds on one model call (fix 9)

The OpenAI latency guide lists the levers: make fewer requests, generate fewer output tokens, run independent steps in parallel, stream results, and "don't default to an LLM" when a cache or lookup will do ([OpenAI latency guide](https://developers.openai.com/api/docs/guides/latency-optimization)). The budget below is measured from when the user stops speaking. Its timings are estimates to be measured, not facts.

| Step | Budget | How |
|---|---|---|
| Final transcript after speech ends | 0.2–0.5 s | Stream audio to the speech service, opening the connection when the mic opens. One vendor reports 150 ms typical (P50) ([AssemblyAI](https://www.assemblyai.com/docs/streaming/benchmarks)) |
| Per-user cache check | 20–50 ms | A cache hit skips the model entirely |
| One structured model call, thinking off, ~80–150 output tokens | 0.6–1.2 s | Candidates are found *before* the call, from words and aliases in the transcript, so one call both parses and picks; short JSON keys; fixed prompt text first so it can be cached |
| One batched database call | 50–150 ms | One SQL function takes the whole item list. That is better than `Promise.all` (JavaScript's version of `asyncio.gather`) over many queries, because it is one round trip |
| Screen and ring | Shown early | Food names appear from the partial transcript; the ring shows a dimmed provisional value, then settles |
| Headroom | ~0.5 s | Run the Edge Function in the same region as the database |

The earlier redesign research set the visible targets: food names on screen within 1.5 s, the ring updated within 2.5 s typically (P50) and within 5 s for the slowest 1 in 20 logs (P95). Thinking must be turned off for parsing. Claude Haiku 5.5 thinks by default and can only disable it at effort "high" or below ([Anthropic models overview](https://platform.claude.com/docs/en/about-claude/models/overview)). Gemini's Flash pricing explicitly includes thinking tokens in the output price ([Gemini pricing](https://aistudio.google.com/docs/pricing)).

### Let search scores decide when to ask (fix 10)

Confidence should come from how cleanly the search matched, not from the model rating itself. No research was found on how well a model's stated confidence about nutrition matches reality. An exact alias hit plus a learned portion counts as high confidence: auto-log with an undo bar. If the top two candidates' RRF scores are close, or the model said "none", show candidate chips. Only for those close calls, run three model calls in parallel and take the majority food ID. That adds cost but little delay, because the calls run at the same time.

### Pick the model last, on STEADY's own numbers (fix 11)

After fixes 2–10, the model's job shrinks to identifying foods and pulling out quantities from messy, multilingual speech. It no longer does nutrition arithmetic. Small current models are probably good enough for that, but nobody has published a benchmark proving it. NutriBench covers only 2024-era models: GPT-4o with step-by-step reasoning scored best at 66.82%, a fine-tuned Gemma2-27B reached 61.71%, and adding a retrieval step did not consistently help ([NutriBench](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)). The bake-off should measure food-ID accuracy, unit accuracy, typical (P50) and slow-case (P90) latency from the Edge Function's region, and the share of repeat runs that come back identical. Run it alongside a speech bake-off on real Hinglish food clips.

### Make photos equal to voice by sending them through the same pipeline (fix 12)

A photo should produce the same `{food, count, unit, size}` output, plus any caption, and then flow through the same search, portion table and per-user memory. If a user's katori has been learned at 120 g, a photo of "1 katori" uses 120 g. Photo grams should be a starting guess with a visible uncertainty range, shown with chips. Models do use nearby objects for scale: GPT-4V used surrounding items as size references in one study ([arXiv 2312.08592](https://arxiv.org/abs/2312.08592)).

Lab results set the ceiling. A depth-sensing phone app reached about **14% weight error and 12.7% energy error** in controlled tests ([JMIR mHealth 2020](https://mhealth.jmir.org/2020/3/e15294/PDF)). Estimating depth from an ordinary single camera reached 14.7% calorie error on the Nutrition5k dataset ([DPF-Nutrition](https://mdpi.com/2304-8158/12/23/4293)). Phone LiDAR is "often insufficient" for small, grainy foods like rice ([arXiv 2602.05078](https://arxiv.org/html/2602.05078v1)). And most Android phones have no depth sensor at all. For photos of Indian cooked food, hidden oil and ghee are the largest invisible error, so the one oil question from the earlier redesign research ("light, normal or heavy tadka?") applies here too.

## ±10% is a promise for known weights, not for first-time photos

Even the "true" numbers are noisy. FAO notes that the different ways of calculating food energy give "myriad possibilities" that differ by a few percent ([FAO](https://www.fao.org/3/Y5022E/y5022e04.htm)). US labels may legally understate calories by up to 20% ([FoodNavigator-USA](https://www.foodnavigator-usa.com/Article/2010/01/06/Low-calorie-foods-could-contain-more-energy-than-labels-state/)). For cooked Indian foods, table values came within ±5% of lab analysis for each food but drifted to about 27% over a whole day ([APJCN](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). Photo systems reach 12–15% only in the lab.

So ±10% is realistic where the gram weight is known: packaged food read from a label or barcode, food weighed in scale mode, and repeat foods with learned portions. In those cases the only error left is the database's few percent. A sensible restatement of the founder's target has five parts, all of them this report's proposals: **100% identical** items and calories for repeated inputs; **signed bias within about ±5% at day level**; a **median error of 10% or less** on known-weight and repeat foods; a **median error of 20% or less** on first-time text and voice; and **photos that are unbiased and within roughly 15–20%**. Protein should be tracked separately against the same targets. Once protein comes from database rows, its accuracy depends only on getting the food and the grams right. "Better than rivals" becomes a checkable claim once the same held-out set has been run through Journable and one or two others.

The gaps are real, and they sit exactly where STEADY differs from everyone else. Several can be closed cheaply by STEADY itself.

| Gap | What is missing | How STEADY can close it |
|---|---|---|
| Reddit | Reddit blocked both the search tool and its public JSON API, so Reddit opinion about Journable is unknown, not absent | Read the relevant subreddits by hand, or ask current users |

## Conclusion

Journable shows that ease alone can earn 4.7 stars and a million downloads while the numbers drift. That is not because accuracy doesn't matter. It is because casual users can't see the errors, and the users who can see them (people who weigh food and track protein) are exactly STEADY's lean-gainer persona. So for STEADY, accuracy is a reason for its target segment to stay, not a lever for store ratings. The sharper finding is that determinism is the cheapest kind of accuracy. When the model only picks from a list and code does the arithmetic, a number can be traced back to a specific row and a specific gram weight. A bad value is fixed once in the data and is fixed for everyone, instead of being chased through prompt changes that may not hold. That is a different engineering discipline from Journable's, and it is what makes "the same meal, the same number" a promise STEADY can put in its marketing.

The race is also not really about which model to use. Every candidate costs under $0.55 per user per month, and after the fixes the model's job is classification, not nutrition. What no vendor has is clean Indian alias and portion data: one canonical dal tadka, a weighed katori, a roti weight per region, and each user's own learned servings. A weekend with a kitchen scale and a validated alias table will likely move STEADY's numbers more than any model upgrade, and it builds the kind of advantage a chat-wrapper competitor cannot copy by switching providers.
