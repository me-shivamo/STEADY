# Lab data pins yields; kitchens still set portions

The best measured evidence for STEADY's weighing sheet is strong on **water and cooking yield** and weak on **piece weight**. Cooked-food moisture is well measured. A Pune lab analysed five replicates of each of 246 Indian foods and meals and found chapati at **34.7% water**, pressure-cooked rice at **73.7%**, home dals at **83–89%** and dry sabzi at **68–71%** ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). European compilations put polished long-grain rice at **2.98× its raw weight across 140 determinations** ([Bognár 2002](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/9642002/1928)). Household measures are now partly measured too. NIN's 2024 guidelines weighed four standard katoris of **115, 155, 200 and 360 ml** ([NIN DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)), and the only Indian study that measured real serving bowls found them at 240–600 ml ([Gupta et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)). No published study we could read gives a weighed mean per piece for Indian roti, paratha, idli or dosa, or a weighed biryani plate. Those defaults have to be rebuilt from NIN's calories per household unit plus measured moisture, which caps them at grade B or C. One number matters more than any default: in rural Pune, **roti weight varied 22% between households but only 3% within one** ([Rao et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980008004424)), so a single per-user calibration beats any population average. Against this evidence, migration 053 ships three portions taken from the 240 ml US measuring cup. Its chai cup is 240 g (118 kcal vs NIN's 75). Its rice cup is 186 g, or 242 kcal per 240 ml, against 204–205 from NIN and USDA. Its dal "cup" is 240 g, while plain "dal" gets no portion at all (177 kcal vs about 110 for the 150 g katori the evidence supports). The 40 g roti is defensible, but it sits about 15% above a home roti (35 g) and a third above a phulka (30 g). Published data can retire about a fifth of the weighing sheet (banana, whey, peanuts, the milk glass and the egg half of the omelette), and it already supplies the katori sizes and the moisture of rice, roti and dal. Everything else needs a kitchen scale or in-app calibration: the household's own roti, how full a katori is served, oil per pot, poha, upma, idli and dosa yields, chai and chaas, and the biryani plate.

## Water drives most published disagreement, and water is well measured

The same roti appears in published tables at anywhere from 202 to 305 kcal per 100 g, and the same dal at 50 to 145. Almost all of that spread is **water and added fat, not composition**. On a dry-matter basis, plain rice agrees within about 4% across INDB, USDA and the Pune lab (our arithmetic on each source's published water and energy). The portion question has the same structure: a "1 katori" or "1 roti" weight means nothing until you know how wet the food is, and the moisture side of the problem is the part the literature has actually measured. The Pune study pressure-cooked its rice and legumes, made breads as rotis, and measured moisture by NIN-manual methods. It also showed that raw-ingredient food tables, once corrected to the measured cooked moisture, matched lab macronutrients within about ±5% for single foods ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). That result is the licence for the whole approach in this report: **know the dry ingredient and the fat per serving, apply a measured moisture, and the cooked weight follows.**

**Rice is the best-characterised yield in the set.** Bognár's German compilation gives polished long-grain rice a weight yield of **2.98 ± 0.05 boiled (n = 140)** and **2.94 ± 0.09 stewed or absorption-cooked (n = 25)**. Parboiled rice runs 2.90–3.04, brown 2.60 and round-grain 3.19, and the "boiled" category includes pressure-boiling ([Bognár 2002](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/9642002/1928)). Yield follows cooked moisture. Raw milled rice holds 9.93% water in IFCT ([IFCT 2017](https://www.nin.res.in/ebooks/IFCT2017.pdf)), so with no solids lost, yield ≈ 0.90 ÷ (1 − cooked water). That gives about **2.8× at USDA's 68% water** ([FDC 2708408](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2708408/portions)), **3.0× at Bognár's ~70%**, and **3.4× at the Pune lab's 73.7%**. Indian pressure-cooker rice is softer and wetter than US rice, so it sits at the top of that range. The "2×" figures that circulate online have three likely explanations. Some are volume ratios ("1 cup raw makes 3 cups cooked"). Some describe firm or parboiled rice cooked al dente, as for biryani before dum. Some come from rice weighed after it cooled and dried. Bognár's own worked example also implies about 6% of solids lost to the water, which matters only if the water is drained. NIN now advises against that: "Do not cook in excess water and discard it" ([NIN DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). Boiling and absorption cooking gave the same yield in Bognár's data (2.98 vs 2.94), so the cooking method matters less than how long the rice is cooked.

**Roti moisture is a grade-A number; roti weight is not.** Four sources put cooked wheat roti at 33–35% water: Pune lab 34.7%, USDA FNDDS 35.4% ([FDC 2707713](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2707713/portions)) and USDA SR 33.0% ([FDC 171844](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171844/measures)). Atta holds 11.1% water ([IFCT 2017](https://www.nin.res.in/ebooks/IFCT2017.pdf)), so each gram of atta becomes about **1.33–1.38 g of cooked roti**, plus any ghee. On that basis, 18 g of atta makes a 25 g roti, 25 g makes 34 g, 30 g makes 41 g and 36 g makes 49 g. On the tawa, dough loses 15–21% of its weight, depending on how soft it was kneaded. That agrees with Bognár's measured 0.89 yield for pan-fried pancakes and 0.90 for baked bread ([Bognár 2002](https://publikationen.bibliothek.kit.edu/9642002/1928)). STEADY's own 053 recalculation turns 80 g atta and 60 g water into 110 g of roti, a yield of 0.79 (`docs/reviews/053-recalc-indian-v1.md`).

**Dals and legume curries split into thickness bands, not one yield.** The Pune lab's pressure-cooked dals measured 84.9% water for toor (68 kcal/100 g), 83.0% for moong (80 kcal) and 89.0% for a chana dal "sauce" (50 kcal). A thick lentil preparation measured 68.6% (142 kcal). Whole kabuli and kala chana curries measured 87.3% and 87.4% ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). Dividing cooked protein by raw-pulse protein, the Pune toor dal carries about **11–12 g of dry dal per 100 g**. A medium home dal as served therefore weighs about **8–9× its dry dal**, a thin one about 10× and NIN's thick dal about 5× (our arithmetic). STEADY's 053 mixed dal reproduces that medium band exactly: 30 g of dry dal becomes 260 g at 84.3% water. The step before the gravy, dry to boiled legume, is poorly sourced. One Brazilian study's lentil index of 3.12 was read only as a search snippet ([DEMETRA](https://www.e-publicacoes.uerj.br/demetra/article/download/84700/53342/337395)). For chickpeas, commercial blogs give 2.2–2.5× ([Country Life Foods](https://countrylifefoods.com/blogs/beans-and-legumes/calculating-chickpeas-dry-to-cooked-weight)). The USDA handbook that tabulates dry-bean yields exists only as an image-only scan ([AH-102](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400525/Data/Classics/ah102.pdf)), and USDA's ARS yield table covers only meat and poultry ([USDA ARS](https://ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400535/Data/retn/USDA_CookingYields_MeatPoultry02.pdf)). For a calorie app this gap matters little, because the served dish's water is measured even though the intermediate step is not.

**INDB's per-piece weights are raw masses, which is why they mislead.** INDB divides the summed raw ingredients by the number of pieces. It applies no cooking loss, keeps all added water, and leaves soak and batter water out of some recipes entirely ([INDB repo](https://github.com/lindsayjaacks/Indian-Nutrient-Databank-INDB-); [Vijayakumar et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11277795/)). The results show it. Its "36 g chapati" is a ball of dough. Its plain dosa reads **381 kcal/100 g** because the batter has no water. Its poha reads 294 kcal because the soak water is missing. Its 300 g rice "plate" is 100 g of rice plus 200 ml of water with nothing evaporated, which matches a 3.0× yield by construction rather than by measurement. INDB is still useful for what it records directly: **flour, fat and filling per piece**. Its chapati uses 20 g atta, its plain paratha about 27 g atta with 10 g oil, and its aloo paratha a filling that makes up about 40% of the raw mass.

## NIN weighed four katoris, and household spoons run 20–40% small

The katori is a utensil, not a unit, and the literature now says so explicitly. NIN's 2024 guidelines list a standard set of twelve cups from 1,500 ml down to 25 ml. NIN says "only 4 cups i.e., C6, C7, C8, C9 were used here as these are most commonly used in households": a **large 360 ml katori, a medium 200 ml, and two small katoris of 155 and 115 ml**. All weights were taken on a Seca Culina 852 kitchen scale. The same document notes that "a difference of 5g in weight is not very apparent in the katori", and that household measures "rely on subjective, categorical size assessments". Its adult meal plans then fix "1 cup/Katori = 200ml" throughout ([NIN DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)).

Survey designers have converged on 200 ml as the nominal katori but not as the measured one. LASI-DAD (4,465 older adults in 22 states) standardised on a 200 ml katori and a 200 ml glass. Its pre-test used three sizes of each utensil, and interviewers found that "confusing and logistically cumbersome" ([LASI-DAD diet methods](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/)). A Kolkata adolescent FFQ chose a 150 ml katori, a 250 ml glass and a 200 ml cup, and its authors said these "do not relate to any national dietary guidelines" ([Rathi et al. 2017](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571590/)). Older NNMB reference bowls, as quoted second-hand, were 105, 140 and 200 ml. The one study that measured real utensils, 58 bowls and 16 glasses across 27 households in rural Palwal, Haryana, found serving vessels of **240, 320 and 600 ml**. It concluded that national reference portions "would have underestimated the food consumed by the child by 55–60%" ([Gupta et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)). The disagreement is mostly definitional. A small steel vati at the thali holds 100–155 ml, the regular eating katori 150–200 ml, and serving and refill bowls 240–600 ml. Nobody has measured how full each is served. Liquids such as dal sit below the brim, while rice, poha and dry sabzi can be mounded above it. NIN's own weighings show why a katori-to-gram rule needs a density step: 20 g of loose spinach fills three-quarters of a 200 ml katori, while 200 ml of dal weighs about 200 g.

**"Cup" means three different volumes in STEADY's own sources.** NIN's cup is 200 ml ([NIN DGI 2011](https://www.nin.res.in/downloads/DietaryGuidelinesforNINwebsite.pdf)). INDB's Units file defines "1 cup = 240ml = 240g" ([INDB repo](https://github.com/lindsayjaacks/Indian-Nutrient-Databank-INDB-)), and USDA uses 240 ml. NIN's caffeine note refers to "a cup (150 ml) of brewed coffee", and the Pune paper's worked day counts "milk 1 cup" as 125 g ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). An Indian teacup is therefore a separate object from the 200 ml survey cup and the 240 ml measuring cup. The resolver needs to know which cup a row's portion refers to.

**Spoons are smaller than their names.** Measuring spoons are 5 and 15 ml. Real household cutlery is not. Sri Lankan households, the nearest culturally similar measured sample (50 homes, 45 teaspoons and 15 tablespoons, each filled to the brim with water and weighed in triplicate), averaged **4.02 ± 0.97 ml per teaspoon and 9.00 ± 3.39 ml per tablespoon**, and 93.3% of spoons held less than nominal ([PLOS GPH 2025](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12880656/)). Greek household spoons ranged from 2.5 to 7.3 ml for teaspoons and 6.7 to 13.4 ml for tablespoons ([Falagas et al. 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1742-1241.2010.02402.x)). Grams per spoon also depend on the food. NIN's weighings give 5 g per teaspoon for rice and dal but only 3.3 g for wheat flour, and NIN rounds oil and ghee to "5 g = 1 teaspoon". USDA's butter oil (ghee) weighs 12.8 g per tablespoon ([FDC 173412](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173412/nutrients)), a density of about 0.87 g/ml, so a level measuring teaspoon holds about **4.3 g of ghee**. NIN's 5 g is a rounded convenience figure. A heaped spoon of semi-solid ghee scooped from a jar could plausibly be 1.5–2× a level one, but nobody has measured it. No source gives a measured volume for a ladle (karchi), a South Indian steel tumbler, or a "plate".

## Item by item: NIN's calories per unit plus measured water set the defaults

The best "Indian portion" evidence turns out to be NIN's per-serving energy values. They encode how much raw cereal or pulse sits in one household unit, and combining that with measured moisture gives a cooked weight. NIN's 2011 Annexure 8 gives **phulka 80 kcal, paratha 150, rice 170 per 200 ml cup, idli 150 for two, dosa 125, poha and upma 270 per cup, plain dhal 100 per ½ cup, dry vegetable 150 per cup and tea 75 per cup**. Its meal plans tie those servings to raw weights, for example "Cereals 120 g → Rice 2 Cups, Pulkas 2" and "Pulses 20 g → Dhal ½ Cup" ([NIN DGI 2011](https://www.nin.res.in/downloads/DietaryGuidelinesforNINwebsite.pdf)). Single-ingredient staples need no reconstruction, because USDA, Indian grading rules and a few field studies weigh them directly. Grades in the tables below mean the following. **A**: several measured sources agree. **B**: one good measured or official source, or several consistent derived ones. **C**: weak, secondary, nominal or reconstructed. Numbers marked "derived" are our arithmetic on the cited values.

**Sources disagree on roti weight because they measure different objects.** INDB's 36 g is dough. USDA's 27, 40 and 52 g are cooked American-recipe rotis indexed by diameter, the only diameter-to-weight data anywhere. USDA's 68 g is a thick packaged roti. NIN's 80 kcal phulka implies about 25 g of atta and 34 g cooked. A thin home phulka from 20–22 g of atta weighs 28–30 g, a North Indian tawa or dhaba roti from 35–40 g of atta weighs 48–55 g, and a "30 g roti" quoted online is sometimes a raw-atta figure labelled as cooked. Rural Maharashtra adds a regional twist: there "roties" are mostly bajra and jowar bhakri, eaten at about twice the daily weight of wheat chapati (285 vs 138 g/day among women who ate each). Those millet rotis are drier than wheat (bajra 28.7% water, jowar 31.2%) ([Rao et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980008004424); [Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). Rao's household finding is the most important fact in this table. Within one home, roti weight barely moves (coefficient of variation 3%), but across homes it moves 22%, and cooked rice servings vary 28.5%. A population default for any given user will typically be off by about a fifth, and **one calibration removes most of that error**.

**Paratha uncertainty is mostly oil.** INDB builds a plain paratha with about 10 g of oil. NIN's 150 kcal fits about 30 g of atta plus 6 g of fat. No study measured oil uptake per paratha at home or at a dhaba. The Pune lab's aloo paratha is the one solid anchor: 55.5% water, 205 kcal and 6 g fat per 100 g. Its filling makes up roughly 35–45% of the weight. STEADY's recalculated aloo paratha (216 kcal, about 91 g per piece) lands within 5% of that lab value.

**Steamed and griddled batters have no measured piece weight anywhere.** The Pune lab's idli (231 kcal) was analysed with coconut chutney, and its dosa (260 kcal) was a masala-type dosa with potato filling, so neither is a plain-item value. The ICVGIP 2025 thali dataset, with gram-level weights for 267 plates and 41 dishes ([ICVGIP Food Scanner](https://cdn.iiit.ac.in/cdn/cvit.iiit.ac.in/images/ConferencePapers/2025/ICVGIP_Food_Scanner.pdf)), the MDRF *Atlas of Indian Foods* ([Dr Mohan's](https://drmohans.com/atlas-of-indian-foods/)) and Lady Irwin College's 313-food North Indian portion tool ([MNM 2023](https://content.iospress.com/articles/mediterranean-journal-of-nutrition-and-metabolism/mnm230039)) are the most likely published sources, but none could be read in this pass. NIN's breakfast plans give the only usable anchor: 70 g of cereal plus 20 g of pulse makes 4 idlis or 3 dosas, so about 22.5 g of dry batter per idli and 30 g per dosa. That puts a plain idli at 37–55 g and a home dosa at 50–60 g, depending on moisture that nobody has measured.

**Poha and upma are where the published densities fail.** The Pune lab's kanda pohe measured 22.3% water and 349 kcal/100 g, far drier than typical home poha. It looks like either a coconut-rich recipe or a sample that dried before analysis. NIN's 270 kcal is per 200 ml cup holding about 50 g of dry poha. At 349 kcal/100 g, that cup would have to weigh only 77 g, far less than a cup of soaked, cooked poha plausibly weighs. NIN therefore points to a home density of roughly 150–200 kcal/100 g. Upma has the same problem: the lab's 48% water is the dry end of a range that reaches about 70% for softer styles. Without a measured dry-to-cooked yield for either dish, both plate weights stay at grade C.

**Biryani is the weakest row in the whole sheet.** INDB has no chicken biryani. Its mutton biryani "plate" of 208 g leaves out the rice-cooking water and codes "4 tbsp" of fat as 4 tsp ([INDB repo](https://github.com/lindsayjaacks/Indian-Nutrient-Databank-INDB-)). With 50 g of raw rice per plate at a 3.0× yield, a home plate would be 250–280 g. The only restaurant numbers come from consumer databases and blogs. No study measured the rice-to-meat split either, so the 45% rice / 35% chicken / 20% sauce split in STEADY's model prompt has no published basis.

| #8 Paneer gravy | **150 g** | 100 / 250 g | water not measured; 40–60 g paneer; fat: palak paneer 6–8 g, butter masala or shahi 12–15 g at home, 20–30 g restaurant | INDB paneer recipes (40–75 g paneer, 7.5–38 g fat ingredients); NIN veg with gravy 170 kcal per cup | C |
| #9 Curd, 1 katori | **150 g** | 100 / 200 g | — | NIN plans portion curd at 75, 100 or 150 ml per meal ("Curd ½ cup"); INDB raita uses 100 g | B for the 100 ml serving, C for katori fill |

**NIN's "½ cup dal = 100 kcal" and the lab's 68 kcal/100 g describe different dals, not a composition disagreement.** NIN's half cup is 100 ml built from 20 g of raw pulse plus 3–4 g of fat, a thick dal. The Pune toor dal holds about 11–12 g of pulse and 1.7 g of fat per 100 g, a thin-to-medium Maharashtrian amti whose extra carbohydrate is consistent with the jaggery and tamarind the paper says were added ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf); [NIN DGI 2011](https://www.nin.res.in/downloads/DietaryGuidelinesforNINwebsite.pdf)). By our arithmetic, about 70% of the 1.45× gap is thickness and 30% is tadka fat. INDB's thin dal (about 50 kcal, all 240 ml of water kept) and USDA's thick lentil dal (145 kcal, 66% water, [FDC 2707427](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/2707427/nutrients)) sit at the two extremes. Per katori, though, the sources converge. A 150 g katori of STEADY's 053 medium dal is 110 kcal, the Pune dal gives about 102, and NIN's 100 ml serving is 100. **A thin / medium / thick selector over a fixed katori captures more real variation than any single per-100 g value.** No source reports how common each thickness is by region, so the medium default is a judgement call, made from the Pune lab median.

**Oil is the unmeasured variable that matters most.** Home recipes put 4–5 g of tadka into a dal serving, and INDB's dal makhani and moti mahal dal carry 19–20 g. That is a 3–5× spread inside one dish name ([INDB recipes](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/lindsayjaacks/Indian-Nutrient-Databank-INDB-/main/recipes.xlsx)). The Pune lab's dry sabzis carry 3.6–5.9 g of fat per 100 g, which is lower than INDB's 5–15 g per bowl, so INDB recipe oil probably sits at the high end of home practice. Population surveys show a regional gradient. NIN's *What India Eats*, as reported by Down To Earth, puts visible fat at about 45.9 g/day in the urban North against 22.9 g/day in the urban South ([Down To Earth](https://www.downtoearth.org.in/news/urban-north-india-has-highest-fat-intake-obesity-73594); [NIN](https://www.nin.res.in/nutrition2020/what_india_eats.pdf)). A comparison of catered and lab-made Indian curries found the catered versions "generally very high" in fat, but we read only the abstract ([Ashwini et al.](https://core.ac.uk/download/328150961.pdf)). No study measured oil per serving in restaurant dal, chole, rajma or paneer butter masala, so every "restaurant" multiplier in this table is an inference.

NIN's dry-vegetable value works as a check on the sabzi defaults. "Dry 1 cup 150 kcal" at the lab's 133 kcal/100 g, or 053's 115, implies a 200 ml cup holding 113–130 g of sabzi, which matches the 120 g default. Paneer gravy is the weakest row: no Indian lab has measured its moisture or fat in either home or restaurant versions.

**Chai is the cleanest convergence in the dataset.** NIN's cup (50 ml toned milk plus 2 tsp sugar) is 75 kcal ([NIN DGI 2011](https://www.nin.res.in/downloads/DietaryGuidelinesforNINwebsite.pdf)). Independently, the Pune lab's tea is 63 kcal/100 g, which makes **76 kcal** in a 120 g cup ([Chiplonkar & Agte 2007](https://apjcn.qdu.edu.cn/16_2_22.pdf)). Two unrelated sources agree within 2% on energy per cup, even though neither states the cup's volume. INDB's chai (25 ml milk and 1 tsp sugar per cup, all 180 ml of water kept) is a lighter recipe at roughly 35 kcal, a real variant rather than an error. No measured source covers kadak, office-vending or street "cutting" chai, whether for cup volume, milk share or sugar.

**Glass, chaas and oats are nominal, not measured.** The 200 ml glass is a survey convention adopted by LASI-DAD and the NMJI North-India FFQ ([LASI-DAD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/); [NMJI 2014](https://nmji.in/nmji/archives/Volume-26/Issue-5/OA-II-26-5-2014.pdf)). Haryana's measured glasses were in a table that could not be read. Chaas rests on a single INDB recipe, and oats on one INDB recipe plus NIN's 30 g cereal portion. For curd, NIN's per-meal planning amount (100 ml) is smaller than what a full 155–200 ml katori holds, so "1 katori dahi" defaults to the same 150 g fill as dal, and "a little curd" or "½ katori" maps to NIN's 100 g.

The banana is the one item where Indian and US weighings meet cleanly. Karnataka's two-season Grand Naine pulp (124.9 g) sits 6% above USDA's medium (118 g edible). An Indian blog that calls USDA's 118 g "unpeeled" has misread the table ([hassanchef](https://www.hassanchef.com/2026/01/calories-in-1-banana-kela-khane-ke-fayde.html)). Variety matters more than size: a Yelakki weighs less than half a Cavendish, so the variety name should switch the default. **Eggs need an in-shell versus edible conversion.** India's Agmark grades are in-shell weights: medium 45–52 g, large 53–59 g ([Agmark rules](https://www.pashudhanpraharee.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/TABLE-EGGS-GRADING-AND-MARKING-RULES.pdf)). An Indian layer handbook gives a 57 g egg as about 50 g of food ([layer handbook](https://www.pashudhanpraharee.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/A-HAND-BOOK-FOR-COMMERCIAL-LAYER-FARMING-IN-INDIA.pdf)), so an Agmark medium yields about 40–46 g edible. NIN's "½ egg = 20 g" fits that, which means 50 g edible is a large egg, not a typical Indian one. The handful default comes from a single New Zealand study of adult hands and Western-sized nuts. Indian Spanish-type groundnut kernels are probably smaller, so an Indian handful could weigh less, but no one has measured it. The whey default depends on the scoop each manufacturer packs, and MuscleBlaze labels some variants with a *heaping* scoop, so brand beats any generic value.

## Migration 053 imports the US cup three times

Three of the four portion values 053 ships are the 240 ml US measuring cup in different forms, and each overstates an Indian serving by 18–60%. Two of them are new in 053 (chai and the rice cup); the 240 g dal cup is inherited from the USDA rows it replaces. The fourth, the 40 g roti, is the only one with real support. The per-100 g recalculations themselves agree with the evidence. The 053 medium dal (74 kcal, 84.3% water, about 11.5 g dry dal and 3.1 g added fat per 100 g) matches the Pune band. The aloo paratha is within 5% of the lab value. The problems sit in the portion rows (`docs/reviews/053-recalc-indian-v1.md`; `.claude/plans/v1.2-recalc-053-spec.md` §(h)).

**Chai's error comes from the cup, not the recipe.** "Half milk, 2 tsp sugar" is a reasonable home recipe, but spread over 240 ml it puts 120 ml of whole milk in each cup. That is 2.4× NIN's 50 ml of toned milk and nearly 5× INDB's 25 ml. Shrinking the cup alone (125 g × 49 kcal = 61 kcal) would quietly halve the sugar to 1 tsp per cup. Re-basing the recipe on a 120 ml cup keeps the founder's 2 tsp and lands at about 77 kcal, matching NIN and the Pune lab. The same 240 ml assumption also sits in STEADY's model prompt ("coffee/tea with milk = one ~240ml cup TOTAL", `supabase/functions/_shared/macroResolver.ts`), so the AI path needs the same fix.

**The rice cup combines a dense cup with a dense rice.** At 130 kcal/100 g, the rice row assumes US-style rice at about 68% water. Paired with USDA's own 158 g cup, it gives 205 kcal per 240 ml, which is NIN's 170 kcal per 200 ml cup scaled up almost exactly. In other words, **volume portions stay consistent when the density and the energy come from the same source.** The 186 g figure was inherited from the existing verified row (5f20d673, USDA FDC 168930). It is 18% heavier than FNDDS's cooked-white-rice cup and contradicts the "1 cup cooked rice ≈ 160g" in STEADY's own model prompt, so check FDC 168930's portion list before shipping it. One more interaction is the founder's to weigh, and the evidence cannot settle it. When a user weighs Indian pressure-cooked rice (73.7% water, 105 kcal/100 g), 130 kcal/100 g overstates it by about 24%. A 250 g plate comes to 325 kcal instead of 263. The 130 value is right for volume logging with a USDA-density cup, and high for weighed soft rice.

**Dal has two problems: a portion that is too large, and an inconsistent one.** The 240 g cup is a US measuring cup, 1.6× the 150 g katori that NIN's 155–200 ml katoris and LASI-DAD's 200 ml standard support. The review file already computes "katori = 150 g (report only)" for every dal, curry and sabzi but does not ship it. Meanwhile `dal`, `mixed dal`, rajma and chole carry no portion, so "1 katori dal" depends on the model's 150 g hint, while "a bowl of daal" can reach the 240 g cup. The resolver maps bowl, cup, glass and mug all to USDA's "cup" (`UNIT_SYNONYMS` in `macroResolver.ts`). The same mapping sends "1 glass milk" and "1 mug chai" to a 240 ml measure wherever a row's portion is a cup, which is 20% above the 200 ml glass that Indian surveys use.

**The roti decision is defensible, and the eval band around it is the bigger problem.** A 40 g roti matches USDA's medium 7-inch and falls well inside Rao's 22% between-household spread, so it is not wrong, just high-centred. But the smoke test s01 ("2 roti", expected 180–300 kcal) excludes NIN's own value for two phulkas (160 kcal). At the evidence-centred 35 g, two rotis come to 171 kcal and would fail. **The eval band should be re-anchored to start near 150 kcal** so it stops rewarding oversized defaults. The roti row's fat (1.1 g/100 g, plain phulka) is also far below the lab chapati's 9.2 g, so a "with ghee" modifier worth about 40–45 kcal per teaspoon matters as much as the piece weight.

Two smaller 053 items touch the sheet. Upma ships at 255 kcal/100 g, the lab's dry 48%-water density. A 150 g katori of it (report only, 382 kcal) is 40% above NIN's 270 kcal cup. Its rava content matches NIN, so the gap is recipe oil and moisture, and a cooking test should settle it before a portion is attached. For poha, the spec's deferral note sets "the lab says 349" against "NIN about 270". Those are per 100 g and per 200 ml cup respectively, and as shown above, NIN actually implies a much lighter home poha.

## A kitchen scale closes what no paper measured

Rao's numbers explain why the weighing sheet cannot simply be replaced. When one home's roti varies 3% and the population varies 22%, the most accurate thing STEADY can do for a given user is learn that user's roti once ([Rao et al. 2009](https://doi.org/10.1017/S1368980008004424)). The Haryana finding, a 55–60% underestimate from reference portions ([Gupta et al. 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)), says the same about bowls. LASI-DAD's experience says to ask once rather than every time, because three utensil sizes per question confused even trained interviewers ([LASI-DAD](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/)). NIN itself concedes that household utensils vary "substantially" ([NIN DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)). The design that follows is a published default for every item, plus a one-time "your roti", "your katori" and "your spoon" calibration. After that, the user's own numbers are reused.

**Published data is good enough to drop several rows.** Banana (#20), whey (#18), peanuts (#21), the milk glass (#16) and the katori sizes rest on measured or label data at grade A or B. Rice yield and moisture, roti moisture, and dal and sabzi moisture bands are settled well enough that a home weighing would add little. These rows can become optional spot checks. The 2-egg omelette (#19) is half settled. The eggs are measured, but the oil is not, so keep the row and record oil only.

**Some gaps only a kitchen scale or the user can close.** No published study measures any of the following:

| Gap | Why published data cannot close it | What to record |
|---|---|---|
| Roti and phulka weight for this household (#1–2) | 22% between-household spread; no Indian mean per piece | 3 rotis on 2 days, plus atta grams before cooking |
| Katori fill by dish (#5–9, #11) | No study measures fill as a share of the brim | Water capacity of each katori and bowl, then the served weight of dal, rice and sabzi |
| Oil per pot (#3, #5–8, #19) | No Indian home vs restaurant measurement; regional visible fat spans about 2× | Oil or ghee per pot and number of servings, as the sheet already asks |
| Poha, upma, idli and dosa yields (#12, #13, #24, #25) | No measured dry-to-cooked yield or plain piece weight; 053 deferred poha, idli and dosa for this reason | Dry ingredient, oil, water and final pot weight; batter per piece and cooked piece weight |
| Biryani plate, home vs restaurant (#23) | Only consumer-database numbers | Home plate weight, plus 2–3 delivery orders with the app's stated calories |
| Chai cup and milk share; chaas dilution (#15, #17) | Energy per cup converges, but volume and milk share are unmeasured | Cup capacity, milk in ml, sugar in tsp; curd to water for chaas |
| "Spoon" of ghee, level vs heaped (#22) | Household spoons measured only in Sri Lanka and Greece; heaped never measured | Ghee weight on the spoon actually used |

Every row in that table is a version of the EuroFIR cooking test: "Use a cooking test to determine your own weight yield factor" ([EuroFIR recipe guideline](https://www.eurofir.org/wp-admin/wp-content/uploads/2015/12/EUROFIR-RECIPE-GUIDELINE_FINAL.pdf)). Each weighing gives STEADY yield factors that no published Indian table contains. The best literature to obtain next, before more weighing, is the ICVGIP 2025 thali dataset, the MDRF *Atlas of Indian Foods*, the Lady Irwin portion tool, NIN's *Nutritive Value of Indian Foods* household-measure tables, an OCR of USDA AH-102, and an FDC API key to pull USDA's sugar and oil spoon weights. Any of these could lift several C rows to B without a scale. FAO/INFOODS sets the order of preference anyway: "calculation of recipes is preferable to taking similar cooked foods", using yield factors ([FAO/INFOODS](https://www.fao.org/infoods/infoods/standards-guidelines/en/)). The data model that fits this evidence stores raw-ingredient anchors per unit (atta, rava, poha, rice, dry dal, fat) plus a moisture parameter. A cooked-gram number alone is not enough, because it cannot follow a user who says "thin", "big" or "with ghee".

## Conclusion

The literature splits the portion problem in two. Water is solved: cooking yields and cooked moisture for roti, rice, dal and sabzi are measured well enough to set STEADY's yield factors today, and the 053 recalculation already uses them correctly. Weight per household unit is not solved, and no further reading will solve it for a given user, because the spread between kitchens is the signal. The most useful quantity the literature offers is not a gram weight but NIN's calories per household unit. Read alongside measured moisture, it reproduces a cooked weight for phulka, paratha, rice, idli, dosa, dal and chai. Where we could test it against a measured density, it agreed within a few percent: chai at 75 vs 76 kcal, and rice at 204 vs 205 kcal per 240 ml.

The second lesson is about where errors enter. The three largest gaps between 053 and the evidence are not composition mistakes; they are the US measuring cup arriving through USDA rows, INDB's unit file and the model prompt. A single explicit rule would remove most of the overstatement before any home weighing happens: an Indian katori is 150 g of dal, an Indian glass is 200 ml, an Indian teacup is about 120 ml, and "cup" always names its volume. After that, the 25-row weighing sheet becomes about a dozen grouped cooking tests and utensil calibrations, the measurements no published source can replace.
