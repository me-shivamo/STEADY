# Food matching methods: robust, measurable matching of user food text to composition-database rows

*Research notes compiled 2026-11-12 for STEADY (Supabase Postgres; INDB + IFCT 2017 + USDA FDC shared verified table). Sources dated inline; "search-snippet level" marks claims not read from the full source. Inferences are opinion/design proposals, not evidence.*

## Q1. FAO/INFOODS Guidelines for Food Matching (v1.2, 2012): core rules

### Takeaway
INFOODS treats matching as a documented, quality-coded decision: match on food identity plus every descriptor that changes composition (preparation state, raw/cooked, fat/water, part, fortification, variety), and when the reported food is generic ("milk", "fish") do NOT pick one row — take a weighted (consumption-share) or arithmetic mean of several plausible rows, or use the most common form in the target population.

### Cited Findings
- Version 1.2 dated November 2012; prepared by Barbara Stadlmayr, Ramani Wijesinha-Bettoni, David Haytowitz, Doris Rittenschober, Judy Cunningham, Renee Sobolewski, Sandra Eisenwagen, Janis Baines, Yasmine Probst, Emily Fitt, U. Ruth Charrondiere. No newer version found (a French translation was published 2015). — [FAO/INFOODS Guidelines for Food Matching v1.2 (PDF)](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf); [FAO Open Knowledge record](https://openknowledge.fao.org/items/e8d25281-ed9d-4b10-9b1e-a1a5384b7838/full)
- Purpose: "Food matching links food consumption/supply data with food composition data"; matching quality is "critical to obtaining high quality estimations of nutrient intakes". — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Step 1: identify the food component of interest (matching decisions depend on it; e.g., salted vs unsalted only matters if sodium is studied). "Never copy only the energy values for energy intake estimations" — macronutrients are needed to compute energy with conversion factors (protein 4, fat 9, available carbohydrate 4, fibre 2, alcohol 7 kcal/g). — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Step 2: prioritise by amount consumed. Quality of intake estimates "is mainly determined by the quality of the food matches of foods consumed in significant quantities"; lower-quality matches are acceptable for infrequently eaten foods. Recommends the USDA "key foods" approach: identify foods contributing 75% of a nutrient's intake and spend matching effort there. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Step 3 (unspecific foods such as "cheese", "milk", "fish"): consider the population's habits (ethnic group, age, rural/urban). "When the cooking method is not indicated … it needs to be estimated by selecting the most common cooking method(s) for the food in the population." Match "the most commonly consumed form of the food (e.g. fortified vs. unfortified)". Example: rural milk → whole milk; urban → semi-skimmed or a mix. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Step 3b: for unspecific foods, match "at least 3 food items" spanning the range of nutrient values; broad categories (e.g., "vegetables") may need 20–30 or more. Value = weighted mean by market share/consumption pattern ("preferred option") or arithmetic mean ("less recommended"). Exception: infrequently consumed foods can use a single match. Avoid matching to a specific brand unless the brand was reported. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Step 4: document every match with a quality code plus the source (FCT edition) and item number. Table 1: **A (high)** = food and all descriptors match exactly AND component definitions meet international standards; **B (medium)** = mean of several items, recipe calculation with yield/retention factors, or similar food of similar botanical origin (≥3 items, excluding extreme values); **C (low)** = very different but closest possible (camel → beef), recipe calc without yield/retention factors, raw values applied to cooked dishes without adjustment, or non-standard component definitions. Optional sub-codes A/A2/B/B2/C/C2 (single vs multiple weighted selections) and D = values from a default table / borrowed FCT. "It is generally better to take an A-match from another FCT than to put a C-match from the FCT of interest." — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Food identification criteria: food name and descriptors; taxonomic/scientific name; water and fat content. Names vary by country (maize/corn, eggplant/aubergine, rockmelon/cantaloupe). "Water is the most important nutrient to check the food description and the concordance between two foods … the water contents always have to be compared"; fat must also be compared for milk, cheese, meat. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- When no exact match: calculate (mean of items, recipe calculation, other forms of same food), estimate/impute (peas from green beans, boiled used for steamed), borrow from other FCTs/literature/labels (document source and code), or use Google/Wikipedia to identify an unknown dish first. If water differs by >10% between borrowed and target food, adjust all nutrients; if fat differs >10%, adjust fat-related components. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Table 2 characteristics that change nutrient values: processing/preparation state (raw, dried, cooked; boiling vs frying; cooking water absorbed, e.g., rice; oil used); colour; biodiversity (variety/cultivar — macronutrients can vary 10-fold and micronutrients up to 1000-fold between varieties); maturity; wild vs domesticated; part (chicken breast vs leg; cassava tuber vs leaves); refuse/edible portion; fortification/enrichment (country-specific). "Users of FCT often apply values of food components for raw foods to prepared foods, without appropriate adjustments … which will lead to major errors." — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Annex 1 worked examples: "Milk, cow, liquid" vs FCT rows "3.5% fat" and "skimmed 1.5%": "Do not just match to only one food item unless the food is infrequently consumed … Calculating a mean will at least reduce the bias." Generic "Milk" with buffalo/cow/goat/chocolate/rice/soy rows: consider cultural aspects ("somewhere else rice and soy milk would not be considered as milk") and exclude implausible items before averaging. "Tomato, pan-fried with olive oil": best solution is a recipe calculation (tomato raw + absorbed oil, with yield/retention factors), not "tomato, raw". "Green leaves, sun-dried" cannot be matched to raw leaves. Unknown dish "Muri" (Bangladesh/India): identify via web search, find a standard recipe, then recipe-calculate. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)

### Inferences
- The INFOODS A/B/C(/D) codes map directly onto a `match_quality` column STEADY could store per log entry (exact INDB dish = A; generic "milk" → weighted default = A2/B2; IFCT raw ingredient used for a cooked dish without yield factor = C). This gives an auditable accuracy signal beyond top-1 hit rate.
- For "milk"/"rice"/"roti" defaults, INFOODS' preferred answer is a population-weighted composite (or the most common form for the population), not whichever row ranks first. For a consumer app where the user sees one row, the practical translation is: curate one "default" row per generic term per region (e.g., India: toned milk / cooked white rice / whole-wheat roti), mark it as a B-quality match, and let the user refine.
- The raw-vs-cooked rule is the single biggest error source for STEADY's mixed table: IFCT rows are raw ingredients; INDB rows are cooked dishes. A text match of "rice" to IFCT raw rice (~350 kcal/100 g) instead of cooked rice (~130 kcal/100 g) would be roughly a 2.5–3× energy error per gram (opinion/order-of-magnitude, based on typical raw vs cooked rice values, not from this source).

### Gaps
- The guidelines are procedural for human compilers; they do not discuss automated/string-matching techniques, synonyms or search ranking.

## Q2. How dietary assessment tools (INDDEX24, Intake24, ASA24, GIFT, myfood24) do matching, synonyms and search

### Takeaway
Validated recall tools solve "what did the user mean" mostly with curated food lists, not clever algorithms: every list entry carries hand-entered synonyms, local/regional names, brand names and hidden search metadata, and the list is kept short and specific. Intake24 South Asia (2,283 items, 50% new South Asian dishes, local names "phonetically entered … in English") ran on 29,113 adults with only 1–2% of North/South Indian recalls needing a "missing food", showing curated synonyms + a moderate-size list works for Indian food.

### Cited Findings
- **Intake24 South Asia (South Asia Biobank)** — Bhagtani D, Amoutzopoulos B, Steer T, et al., *Current Developments in Nutrition* 2025;9(2):104543. Food database of 2,283 items: 1,131 (49.6%) retained from the UK Intake24 database, 1,152 (50.4%) new South Asian foods and dishes, sourced from local dietary surveys, recipe books, FCDBs, markets and local knowledge. Regional teams supplied local names, which were "phonetically entered into the food database in English". Nutrients came from UK Nutrient Databank codes (~10,000) using a "best basic" match, not local recipes (no Sri Lankan FCDB; Indian FCDB had 528 items). — [PMC11847516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11847516/)
- Intake24 South Asia performance (n = 29,113 recalls): median completion 13 min (IQR 10–18); recalls with ≥1 "missing food" (not found in search, manual coding needed) = 2,438 (8%) overall — Bangladesh 6%, Pakistan 4%, Sri Lanka 19%, **North India 2%, South India 1%**. The paper does not describe the search algorithm. — [PMC11847516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11847516/)
- Intake24 states it uses "custom search algorithms, synonyms and metadata" to help users find foods. — [intake24.com](https://intake24.com/)
- Intake24 Localisation Toolkit (food list guidance): "The Synonyms column should be used to enter alternative names for the same food item" (e.g., bread roll → buns, baps, cobs, stottie, batch); "Local names were also included as synonyms"; brand names go into synonyms or the food name; metadata can hold "synonyms or regional variations" that "do not display in the food name … but do result in that food being returned" (searching Braeburn returns "Red apple"); where users would not recognise another region's name, "duplicate the entry". Lists should be specific yet short; for less common foods "the most frequently consumed item could be included e.g. Gala apple"; similar foods can be collapsed ("fried rice with seafood"). Users can also browse by category. — [Intake24 Localisation Toolkit: Food Lists](https://actionlabnet.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/ILT/pages/1605967/Food+Lists+-+detailed+guidance)
- Intake24's food database keeps a simplified local description with diacritics removed so foods can be found by typing plain English-keyboard characters (an accent-folding step, i.e., what Postgres `unaccent` does). — [Intake24 v3 docs: food database reference](https://v3.docs.intake24.org/references/food-database.html) (via search snippet; page not fetched in full)
- **Intake24 search engine (open-source code, intake24/intake24, current v4)** — primary-source detail on how a validated recall tool searches. Default survey search settings: `sortingAlgorithm: 'popularity'`, `matchScoreWeight: 20`, `spellingCorrectionPreference: 'phonetic'`, `enableEditDistance: true`, `enablePhonetic: true`, `minWordLength1: 3` (min word length for 1 edit), `minWordLength2: 6` (for 2 edits), `minWordLengthPhonetic: 3`, `firstWordCost: 0`, `wordOrderCost: 4`, `wordDistanceCost: 1`, `unmatchedWordCost: 8`, `maxResults: 100`. A "pairwise associations (machine learning)" ranking ('paRules') is listed but commented as not implemented. — [intake24 search-settings.ts](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/common/src/surveys/search-settings.ts)
- Intake24 ranking = combination of (a) "Food ordering … typically prioritising items based on how frequently they are reported. The more commonly reported foods are listed at the top" (per-locale popularity counters updated with each recall submission; "global popularity" across locales is "not recommended because in practice food codes are rarely shared across locales"; or a fixed predetermined priority) and (b) "Match quality" = a match *cost* summed from penalties: first word not found, word-order violations ("boiled potato" vs "Potato, boiled"), word-adjacency violations ("sweet chicken" vs "sweet and sour chicken"), and each unmatched word ("boiled chicken" vs "sweet and sour chicken" — still matches, but penalised). A slider weights popularity vs match quality. — [intake24 admin i18n strings (surveys.json)](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/i18n/src/admin/en/surveys.json); [ranking code](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/tree/main/apps/api/src/food-index/ranking)
- Intake24 spelling correction applies "when an exact word match cannot be found": edit distance (Levenshtein) up to 2, restricted by word length because short-word corrections mislead ("correcting 'cake' to 'sage' (edit distance 2) is unlikely to be helpful"); and phonetic encoding (English backend uses Metaphone 3; French and Portuguese have their own phonetic backends). "Preferred" method is tried first and the other only if no match, or "Both" combined. — [intake24 surveys.json](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/i18n/src/admin/en/surveys.json); [english-language-backend / metaphone3.ts](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/tree/main/apps/api/src/food-index/language-backends/en)
- Intake24 locales also maintain "synonym sets" (each entry a "space delimited list of synonyms"), "split words" and "split lists" (locale-level tables for splitting compound inputs; their exact semantics were not documented in the excerpt read) and recipe foods. — [Intake24 locale admin docs](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/apps/docs/admin/locales/index.md)
- Intake24's English backend stems with a *truncated* Porter stemmer: step 4 is deliberately skipped with the code comment "step4 in the Porter algorithm is too aggressive for our use case (for example vegetable -> veget)". This is the same failure class as STEADY's english-stemmer problem (idli→"id", chaas→"chaa"). — [intake24 english-language-backend.ts](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/apps/api/src/food-index/language-backends/en/english-language-backend.ts)
- Intake24 matching is non-conjunctive: unmatched query words add a cost rather than excluding the food (contrast with STEADY's current AND-of-all-terms tsquery). — [intake24 surveys.json](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/i18n/src/admin/en/surveys.json)
- Intake24 is experimenting with semantic search: the `foodSearcher` repo (last commits Jan 2026) is a "proof-of-concept" searching Intake24's `foods` table with embeddings in PostgreSQL + pgvector (default `Xenova/all-MiniLM-L6-v2` 384-d; bge-small/bge-m3/embeddinggemma/gemini-embedding-001 supported), evaluated with MRR@1/@10/@50, plus experimental Gemini (2.5-flash-lite) "search hints". No results published in the README. — [intake24/foodSearcher](https://github.com/intake24/foodSearcher)
- Intake24's iterative development (Simpson et al., *Nutrients* 2017;9(2):118, 80 users aged 11–24): search improvements were mainly content changes — "some of the foods and food categories were re-named to be more user-friendly", brand names added, sandwich synonyms ("roll", "butty", "wrap") trigger a "Build my sandwich" option; "missing foods and drinks … were added to the database at regular intervals." — [PMC5331549](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5331549/)
- **myfood24-Germany** ("Adaptation and Evaluation of Myfood24-Germany", *Nutrients* 2020;12(1):160): 11,501 items (7,203 generic, 4,298 branded); screen recordings showed that "finding and choosing an appropriate item within the database was a major challenge"; filters by category/brand exist. — [PMC7019560](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7019560/) (via search snippet)
- **INDDEX24** (Tufts INDDEX Project; transitioned to the Intake Center for Dietary Assessment at FHI Solutions on 1 June 2022): food and recipe names can be translated into up to 4 languages; foods can be coded with FoodEx2 and INFOODS tagnames; national food/recipe/ingredient lists ("FRIL") were compiled from prior local surveys and FCTs and reviewed by national experts (Burkina Faso, Viet Nam). No dedicated synonym-matching feature documented. — [INDDEX24 platform](https://inddex.nutrition.tufts.edu/inddex24); [INDDEX24 platform resources](https://inddex.nutrition.tufts.edu/node/303); [Coates slides, MRC Epid 2020](https://www.mrc-epid.cam.ac.uk/wp-content/uploads/2020/07/Jennifer-Coates_Slides.pdf)
- **ASA24** uses USDA FNDDS composition data (same as NHANES); ASA24-2018 kept FNDDS 2011-12 foods and added "new, popular foods from FNDDS 2013-14"; researchers can reassign codes outside the system and recalculate. — [ASA24 database changes](https://epi.grants.cancer.gov/asa24/respondent/database-changes.html); [ASA24 researcher analysis](https://epi.grants.cancer.gov/asa24/researcher/analysis.html)
- FNDDS (the database behind ASA24 and NHANES) handles under-specified reports with NFS/NS codes (see Q7 for the derivation rule). FNDDS 2019-2020 contains 5,624 food and beverage items (4,982 foods / 642 beverages) — an order of magnitude smaller than FDC overall, which is part of why ASA24/NHANES coding is tractable. — [FNDDS 2019-2020 documentation](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2019_2020_FNDDS_Doc.pdf)

### Inferences
- The proven pattern for Indian dishes is a curated alias table (synonyms, regional names, romanisation variants, brand names) attached to each canonical food row, searched alongside the canonical name. Intake24's 1–2% "missing food" rate in India came from content curation, not from an ML matcher.
- The "duplicate entry vs hidden metadata" distinction is useful for STEADY: aliases that users recognise (roti/chapati/phulka) should be searchable and may be shown; aliases they would not recognise should be hidden search metadata.

### Gaps
- Intake24's published papers do not report search accuracy (top-1 hit rates); the algorithm details above come from source code and admin help text, not peer-reviewed evaluation. Whether the South Asia locale uses phonetic correction tuned for romanised Hindi (Metaphone 3 is English-oriented) was not established.
- ASA24's respondent-side search ranking algorithm is not publicly documented in the sources found.
- No technical description of myfood24's ranking or of FAO/WHO GIFT matching techniques was found (GIFT is a data-sharing platform; its food matching is done by contributing surveys).

## Q3. Food description/classification systems (LanguaL, FoodEx2, FoodOn): do they help practical matching, and at what cost?

### Takeaway
Faceted description systems encode exactly the attributes INFOODS says drive nutrient differences (raw/cooked, method, part, form), but full coding is slow (~10 min per item by an expert for FoodEx2) and automated coders reach only ~79% correct descriptions. The practical 2026 pattern is a small closed set of facets (cooked state, cooking method, form, preservation, dish type) extracted by an LLM, not full LanguaL/FoodEx2 coding.

### Cited Findings
- LanguaL is a multilingual faceted thesaurus; foods (other than supplements) are described with descriptors from 14 facets arranged broader→narrower; administered by the European LanguaL Technical Committee since 1996; EuroFIR adopted it, trained compilers, and quality-checked indexing for reproducibility and correctness; >27,000 foods in European FCDBs were LanguaL-indexed for EuroFIR search. FoodEx2 was incorporated into LanguaL 2017; FoodOn "is based largely on LanguaL". — [langual.org](https://langual.org/); [EuroFIR food identification & description](https://www.eurofir.org/?p=19); [EuroFIR LanguaL 2009 thesaurus (PDF)](https://www.eurofir.org/wp-admin/wp-content/uploads/LanguaL/LanguaL%202009%20Thesaurus%20-%20Final.pdf)
- FoodOn's vocabulary originated by transforming LanguaL into an OWL ontology and it carries FoodEx2 terms (labelled "(efsa foodex2)"); LanguaL facet codes for FoodEx2 descriptors are identical to EFSA FoodEx2 codes. — [AgroPortal FoodOn entry](https://agroportal.lirmm.fr/ontologies/FOODON/http%3A%2F%2Fpurl.obolibrary.org%2Fobo%2FFOODON_03401895/serialize/xml); [ICBO 2020 abstract](https://www.informatik.uni-leipzig.de/~loebe/tmp/ICBO2020/abstractW.pdf) (search-snippet level)
- **StandFood** (Eftimov, Korošec, Koroušić Seljak, *Nutrients* 2017;9(6):542): FoodEx2 "consists of 32 facets that can be either implicit or added". Automated FoodEx2 coding (ML classifier for raw/derivative/simple composite/aggregated composite + POS-tagging/lemmatisation + Jaccard/Laplace-weighted description matching) achieved 89% (89.28%) classification accuracy on 532 new items and 79% correct descriptions ("79% of the instances obtain relevant FoodEx2 codes"); overall 79%. Manual coding is "laborious and requires good knowledge of the system": ~10 minutes per item for an expert, i.e., ~11 eight-hour days for 532 items; StandFood took ~5 minutes but checking its output took one working day. — [PMC5490521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5490521/)
- INDDEX24 supports FoodEx2 coding and INFOODS tagnames as optional metadata for harmonisation, not as the search mechanism. — [INDDEX24 platform](https://inddex.nutrition.tufts.edu/inddex24)
- NDS (2026) indexes every food with an LLM-extracted closed-vocabulary facet set — "base food, cooked state, cooking method, form, preservation, coating, dish type, and preparation additives" — and scores candidates 0.70·cosine + 0.30·facet agreement; reranking emphasises "preparation, physical form, defining ingredients, and brand"; typed crosswalk relations exact/broad/narrow/close/no-match. — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- Morales-Garzón et al. show "one description may admit defensible candidates at several levels of specificity" — i.e., hierarchy matters for deciding between generic and specific rows. — cited in [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)

### Inferences
- Full LanguaL/FoodEx2 coding of STEADY's table is not cost-justified for v1: at ~10 expert-minutes per row (StandFood figure), 5,000 rows ≈ 830 hours. A 6–8-facet subset (state raw/cooked; method; form; fat level; fortified; dish vs ingredient; region of origin) can be LLM-extracted from names and INDB/IFCT metadata, then spot-checked — this captures most of the INFOODS Table 2 risk (raw vs cooked especially).
- Using FoodEx2/LanguaL *codes* as a later interoperability layer (e.g., to join INDB, IFCT, USDA rows for the same base food) is plausible, but no source here shows it improves end-user search accuracy directly.

### Gaps
- No head-to-head study found of LanguaL vs FoodEx2 vs FoodOn for improving search/matching accuracy.
- Whether FoodOn's synonym annotations cover Indian dish names (idli, uttapam, chaas) was not checked.

## Q4. Automated / AI food-to-database matching: methods and published accuracy

### Takeaway
The best-documented 2026 systems converge on the same recipe: LLM parses text into a base food + closed-vocabulary facets (cooked state, method, form), hybrid retrieval (lexical full-text + embeddings, fused with RRF k=60), then one listwise LLM rerank with an explicit "no match" option. On 1,000 NHANES-derived meals this reached top-1 0.872 recall / F1 0.875 against exact FNDDS codes and recall@5 0.942; grounding in database rows beat direct GPT-4o estimation on NutriBench (84.6% vs 66.8% within 7.5 g carbs). Similarity thresholds alone do not separate match from no-match.

### Cited Findings
- **Nutrition Data Service (NDS)** — Lin Liao, Peng Li (8up.ai), arXiv 2608.10363, 14 Aug 2026 (preprint; authors are a commercial vendor). Architecture: PostgreSQL + pgvector retrieval index; each description has an embedding, a normalised name for lexical search, and structured facets "base food, cooked state, cooking method, form, preservation, coating, dish type, and preparation additives". Pipeline: (1) LLM with a structured schema parses the query into base food + closed-vocabulary facets (fallback: normalised description); (2) semantic channel (cosine on base-food embedding, deployed score = 0.70·cosine + 0.30·facet agreement; unknown candidate facets get half credit; unspecified query facets ignored) and lexical channel (full-text on normalised names) fused by Reciprocal Rank Fusion, score Σ 1/(k + rank), k = 60; semantic retrieves up to 250, lexical up to 25; (3) one listwise LLM rerank over the top 25 fused candidates "with emphasis on preparation, physical form, defining ingredients, and brand", returning ≤5; "If no candidate is defensible, NDS returns an explicit unsupported result rather than silently substituting the nearest food." Models: gemini-3.6-flash (parse + rerank), 1,536-d gemini-embedding-2-preview, HNSW cosine index. — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- NDS evaluation on 1,000 held-out meal descriptions generated from NHANES recalls (3,597 reference FNDDS codes): strict source-identifier recall@5 = 0.942, top-1 precision 0.879, recall 0.872, F1 0.875; equivalence-aware (siblings with matching energy/macros credited) F1 0.914. "Much of the gap is a choice among dense FNDDS variants distinguished by venue, packaging, or another attribute omitted from the meal text … error is therefore driven more by ranking among near-duplicate records than by failure to retrieve." — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- NDS on all 11,857 NutriBench v1 queries: Acc@7.5 g carbs 84.6%, MAE 4.3 g, answer rate 96.4%, vs best published GPT-4o CoT 66.8%, MAE 8.6 g, answer rate 99.2% (unpaired comparison; the 3.6% unanswered were "mostly foreign meals"). — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- **Lemay et al. 2026** ("Evaluation of large language models for mapping dietary data to food databases", *J Nutr* 156(8):101678, doi:10.1016/j.tjnut.2026.101678) "make abstention a first-class outcome and show that similarity thresholds do not cleanly separate matches from non-matches". Benchmark: 1,304 NHANES ingredient descriptions labelled match/no-match against 256 Davis Food Glycopedia 2.0 foods (693 matchable, 611 no-match). Published system accuracy 0.654 overall (0.822 matchable, 0.466 no-match); NDS 0.688 (0.745 / 0.624). Of NDS's 177 errors on matchable foods, 139 were abstentions; emitted targets agreed with the benchmark 93.1% of the time. NDS deliberately refused raw→cooked transfers (raw broccoli ↛ steamed broccoli florets). — reported via [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363) (secondary; the J Nutr paper itself was not retrieved)
- **NutriMatch** — Jankelow A, …, Segal E, *npj Digital Public Health* 1(1), 2026, doi:10.1038/s44482-025-00001-7: LLM normalises records → embeddings → cosine-similarity matching → LLM-as-judge verifies equivalence (replacing manual expert review); applied to USDA SR Legacy, FNDDS, Tzameret (Israel), MEXT (Japan), Bahrain FCT, AUSNUT; supports multi-language alignment; expanded nutrient coverage from 21→151 (Israeli cohort) and 43→151 (Australian PREDICT). Accuracy figures not retrieved. — [npj Digital Public Health (via aggregator)](https://scour.ing/@hello/p/https://www.nature.com/articles/s44482-025-00001-7)
- **Epicure** (arXiv 2604.22776, 2026): three-layer pipeline — rule-based matching, then embedding similarity, then LLM validation (Gemini 2.5 Flash picks a candidate or "no_match") — matched 712/1,032 ingredients (69.0%) to USDA FoodData Central; unmatched items were mainly culturally specific foods and composite products. — [arXiv 2604.22776](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.22776)
- Eftimov & Koroušić Seljak (2015): POS-tagging + probability-weighted matching of internet recipe ingredients to a food composition database reported 91.82% of ingredients matched (whether top-1 accuracy is unclear from the abstract). — [SciTePress 2015](https://www.scitepress.org/Papers/2015/56123/56123.pdf)
- AI-assisted assignment of glycemic index to USDA food codes: initial AI accuracy 75.0%, but only 31.3% of matches retained after manual curation — evidence that unreviewed LLM matches overstate quality. — [GI database development PDF](https://www.sochob.cl/web1/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Development-of-a-national-database-for-dietary-glycemic-index-and-load-for-nutritional-epidemiologic-studies-in-the-United-States.pdf) (search-snippet level; not fetched in full)
- Morales-Garzón et al. (IPMU 2020) used word embeddings + fuzzy logic to map food composition databases and showed one description may admit defensible candidates at several levels of specificity. — cited in [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363); original [doi:10.1007/978-3-030-50143-3_50](https://doi.org/10.1007/978-3-030-50143-3_50)
- **NutriBench** (Hua, Dhaliwal, Pullela, Burke, Qin; ICLR 2025; arXiv 2407.12843 v6 revised 3 Mar 2026): 11,857 meal descriptions from WWEIA (US) and FAO/WHO GIFT (10 more countries incl. India — 18 Indian meals, 16 unique items — and Sri Lanka), natural-serving and metric-serving variants. 12 LLMs tested with Base, CoT and RAG. Best: GPT-4o CoT 66.82% Acc@7.5 g carbs (answer rate 99.16%); best open model Llama 3.1-405B RAG+CoT 59.89%; 12-model averages 43.24% Base, 47.46% CoT. RAG used "Retri-DB" built from USDA FDC (450,182 items, 13,048 with natural serving sizes): LLM parses meal into components, nearest-neighbour semantic search retrieves nutrition facts per component; natural-serving queries benefited less because retrieved entries are mostly per 100 g. By country (GPT-4o CoT), MAE lowest for Nigeria (2.20 g) and highest for Sri Lanka (15.12 g); error rises with number of items per meal. — [NutriBench arXiv 2407.12843v6](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)
- Požarnik Vavken, Ogrinc, Eftimov, Koroušić Seljak (Jožef Stefan Institute), arXiv 2603.09704v2 (Mar 2026): LLMs (Gemini-2.0-Flash, GPT-4o, Mistral Medium 3, Claude-Sonnet-4) translating Slovene nutrition queries into metadata filters over 32,000 foods embedded with gemini-embedding-001: F1 ≈ 0.99–1.00 on easy/medium questions but only 0.37–0.45 on hard ones; no BM25/hybrid comparison. — [arXiv 2603.09704v2](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.09704v2)

### Inferences
- STEADY's current "LLM parses → Postgres FTS" pipeline is the first stage of the NDS design; the missing pieces are (a) facets (cooked state etc.) as structured columns, (b) a second retrieval channel (trigram and/or embeddings), (c) RRF fusion, (d) an LLM rerank/abstain step over a short slate. All four run inside Supabase Postgres + one cheap LLM call.
- Retrieval recall (is the right row in the top-k?) and ranking precision (is it top-1?) should be measured separately: NDS shows a ~7-point gap between them, driven by near-duplicate rows. STEADY's mixed USDA+INDB+IFCT table will have the same near-duplicate problem (e.g., several "rice" rows), so a deterministic default/tie-break rule matters as much as retrieval.
- Abstention ("no defensible match → fall back to AI estimate or ask the user") is now treated in the literature as a measured outcome, not a failure. STEADY's existing cache→USDA→AI-estimate fallback chain already has this shape.

### Gaps
- NutriBench's per-model Base/CoT/RAG grid is only in a figure, so the isolated effect of RAG for GPT-4o is not quantified in text.
- The Lemay et al. J Nutr 2026 paper was not retrieved directly; numbers above are as reported by NDS.
- No published accuracy numbers found for matching Hinglish/Indian dish text to INDB/IFCT.

## Q5. Hybrid search for short food queries in Postgres/Supabase (full-text + trigram + vector, RRF)

### Takeaway
Postgres already contains every primitive needed: `pg_trgm` (`word_similarity`/`strict_word_similarity`, GIN/GiST indexes) handles typos, Hinglish spelling variants and '/'-joined names (non-alphanumerics are word separators); the `simple` text-search config avoids stemming damage; synonym/thesaurus dictionaries can protect words like "idli" from the English stemmer; Supabase documents an RRF hybrid function. IR evidence says fusion beats single retrievers on average (RRF, TREC), lexical beats dense on rare entity names (dish names are entity-like), but RRF is sensitive to k and a tuned convex combination can beat it.

### Cited Findings
- **pg_trgm (PostgreSQL 18 docs)**: `similarity(a,b)` ∈ [0,1] on shared trigrams; `word_similarity(a,b)` = greatest similarity between a and "any continuous extent" of b's ordered trigrams (≈ best substring match); `strict_word_similarity` requires extent boundaries at word boundaries ("best for whole-word similarity"). Example: `word_similarity('word','two words')` = 0.8; `strict_word_similarity('word','two words')` = 0.571429. Default thresholds: `pg_trgm.similarity_threshold` 0.3 (`%`), `word_similarity_threshold` 0.6 (`<%`), `strict_word_similarity_threshold` 0.5 (`<<%`). Distance operators `<->`, `<<->`, `<<<->` support KNN ordering with GiST only (not GIN). — [PostgreSQL docs: pg_trgm](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/pgtrgm.html)
- pg_trgm trigram extraction: "Non-word characters (non-alphanumerics) are ignored", so they act as word separators; each word is padded with two leading and one trailing space; `'foo|bar'` → trigrams of "foo" and "bar" separately. Similarity is case-insensitive in a default build. GIN (`gin_trgm_ops`) and GiST (`gist_trgm_ops(siglen=…)`, default siglen 12 bytes) indexes accelerate `%`, `<%`, `LIKE/ILIKE` (not left-anchored) and regex; patterns with no extractable trigrams fall back to full index scans. — [PostgreSQL docs: pg_trgm](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/pgtrgm.html)
- pg_trgm docs recommend a spelling-suggestion pattern: build a table of unique words using `ts_stat` over `to_tsvector('simple', …)` ("so the words stay unstemmed"), GIN-trigram-index it, and look up similar words to correct the user's terms before full-text search. — [PostgreSQL docs: pg_trgm](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/pgtrgm.html)
- **Text search dictionaries**: the `simple` dictionary lowercases and drops stop words, returning the lower-cased word otherwise (no stemming). A **synonym dictionary** placed before `english_stem` overrides stemming: "to prevent an English stemmer dictionary from reducing the word 'Paris' to 'pari'. It is enough to have a `Paris paris` line in the synonym dictionary and put it before the `english_stem` dictionary" (file format: one word + synonym per line; trailing `*` = prefix). A **thesaurus dictionary** "replaces all non-preferred terms by one preferred term" with phrase support (requires reindexing on change). A Snowball stemmer "recognizes everything … so it should be placed at the end of the dictionary list". Filtering dictionaries such as `unaccent` can be placed before others to strip accents. — [PostgreSQL docs: text search dictionaries](https://www.postgresql.org/docs/current/textsearch-dictionaries.html)
- **Supabase hybrid search guide**: `hybrid_search(query_text, query_embedding, match_count, full_text_weight=1, semantic_weight=1, rrf_k=50)`; full-text CTE uses `fts @@ websearch_to_tsquery(query_text)` ranked by `ts_rank_cd`; semantic CTE ranks by inner product `<#>`; each CTE returns `least(match_count,30)*2` rows; fused by full outer join with score `coalesce(1.0/(rrf_k + rank_ix), 0.0) * weight` summed; indexes: GIN on the tsvector, HNSW on the embedding. The example tsvector uses `to_tsvector('english', content)`. — [Supabase docs: Hybrid search](https://supabase.com/docs/guides/ai/hybrid-search)
- **RRF origin**: Cormack, Clarke & Büttcher, SIGIR 2009, "Reciprocal rank fusion outperforms Condorcet and individual rank learning methods": score = Σ 1/(k + rank); k = 60 fixed in a pilot and found near-optimal but "not critical"; across four TREC collections RRF beat every individual system and Condorcet fusion (reported MAP ≈ 0.2145 vs 0.2074 Condorcet vs 0.2016 best single system, per search-result summary). — [Cormack et al. 2009 (PDF)](https://plg.uwaterloo.ca/~gvcormac/cormacksigir09-rrf.pdf); [Google Research listing](https://research.google/pubs/pub36196/)
- **Counter-evidence on RRF**: Bruch, Gai & Ingber, "An Analysis of Fusion Functions for Hybrid Retrieval" (ACM TOIS 2023, arXiv 2210.11934): a convex combination (CC) of normalised lexical and semantic scores outperforms RRF in-domain and out-of-domain; "RRF is sensitive to its parameters"; tuned RRF generalises poorly; CC's single weight can be tuned with only a few labelled examples. — [arXiv 2210.11934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934)
- **Lexical vs dense on short/entity queries**: BEIR (Thakur et al., NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks; 18 datasets, 10 systems): "BM25 is a robust baseline"; dense retrievers often underperform out-of-domain; rerankers/late-interaction best but costly. Sciavolino et al. (EMNLP 2021) "Simple Entity-Centric Questions Challenge Dense Retrievers": BM25 72.0% vs DPR 49.7% top-20 accuracy on EntityQuestions (but DPR beats BM25 on Natural Questions, 80.1 vs 64.4). — [BEIR arXiv 2104.08663](https://arxiv.org/abs/2104.08663); [Sciavolino et al. arXiv 2109.08535](https://arxiv.org/abs/2109.08535)
- **Food-specific hybrid in Postgres**: NDS (2026) runs exactly this pattern on PostgreSQL + pgvector: lexical full-text channel (≤25 results) + facet-adjusted semantic channel (≤250) fused by RRF k=60, then LLM listwise rerank over 25 → ≤5, with abstention; top-1 F1 0.875, recall@5 0.942 on FNDDS. — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- Intake24's production ranking is not RRF but a weighted blend of popularity (reporting frequency) and lexical match cost with typo/phonetic correction. — [intake24 surveys.json](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/i18n/src/admin/en/surveys.json)

### Inferences
- Each of STEADY's four symptoms maps to a known Postgres fix: (1) stemmer mangling Hinglish → index names with the `simple` config (or a custom config: `unaccent` → synonym dictionary of protected Hinglish words → `simple`), keep `english` only as a secondary channel if at all; (2) "chapati/roti" as one token → this is a tokenisation artefact of the current pipeline; normalise `/`, `-`, `,` to spaces in a `search_name` column and also store each slash-part as an alias row; pg_trgm already treats `/` as a separator; (3) AND-of-all-words → use OR semantics (e.g., build a `to_tsquery` with `|`, rank by `ts_rank_cd`) or rely on `word_similarity(query, name)` which degrades gracefully ("plain uttapam" vs "Uttapam" scores high on word similarity of "uttapam"); (4) non-relevance ordering → explicit `ORDER BY` fused score, then a tie-break on a curated default/popularity column.
- A 2-channel lexical hybrid (FTS-simple + trigram) fused by RRF is a zero-new-infrastructure first step; pgvector embeddings are a second step whose value on short dish names is uncertain (BEIR/Sciavolino suggest dense retrieval is weakest exactly on rare named entities like "uttapam"), so it should be justified by STEADY's eval harness, not assumed.
- Because Bruch et al. find RRF sensitive to k and CC tunable with few labels, STEADY's existing eval set can be used to choose between RRF (k≈50–60) and a weighted sum; neither should be picked by default.
- `unaccent()` is not marked IMMUTABLE in stock Postgres, so it is commonly wrapped in an immutable SQL function before use in index expressions/generated columns (practitioner knowledge; not verified against docs in this pass). Indian romanisations rarely have diacritics, so `unaccent` matters more for global expansion (e.g., "crème", "jalapeño") than for India.

### Gaps
- No published benchmark found that specifically tests hybrid (lexical + trigram + vector) retrieval on short food-name queries; NDS is the closest and is a vendor preprint.
- Cormack 2009 MAP numbers above are from a search-result summary, not read from the PDF table.

## Q6. Transliteration, Hinglish and Indic normalisation

### Takeaway
Romanised Hindi has no spelling standard, and general multilingual encoders align romanised code-mixed text poorly with both English and Hindi; transliteration models top out around 56–70% top-1 on romanised→native conversion. The robust, cheap approach evidenced by Intake24 South Asia is curated romanised aliases per dish, backed by phonetic/edit-distance tolerance; ML transliteration and Hinglish embeddings are useful as candidate generators, not as canonicalisers.

### Cited Findings
- Intake24 South Asia: local food names supplied by regional teams were "phonetically entered into the food database in English" (i.e., romanised aliases), and the tool achieved 1–2% missing-food rates in North/South India. — [PMC11847516](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11847516/)
- **Aksharantar / IndicXlit** (AI4Bharat; EMNLP Findings 2023, arXiv 2205.03018): 26 million transliteration pairs, 21 Indic languages, 12 scripts. IndicXlit raised Dakshina average top-1 accuracy from 51.8% to 60.5%; on the Aksharantar test set micro-averaged top-1 = 56.31%, frequent words 69.70%, foreign named entities 38.34%; reranking with a word-level unigram language model added ~12% on Dakshina. Open source (AI4Bharat/IndicXlit). — [arXiv 2205.03018](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.03018v2)
- **Code-mixed text in multilingual encoders** (Mazumder, Pathak, Kodali, Patro — IISER Bhopal & Microsoft; arXiv 2603.19771, 20 Mar 2026): last-layer cross-lingual retrieval accuracy EN↔code-mixed(romanised Hinglish): mBERT 54.75/42.90, XLM-R 50.56/50.67; HI↔CM: mBERT 26.30/39.05, XLM-R 38.37/40.46. Code-mixed text sits "loosely" between both languages; continued pre-training on romanised Hinglish alone improves EN↔CM but degrades EN↔HI (Hing-RoBERTa EN→HI 15.02); keeping Devanagari Hindi as an anchor gives the best model (Hing-RoBERTa-Mixed EN↔CM 86.95/92.37, HI↔CM 72.88/77.78). LaBSE, multilingual-e5, MuRIL were not tested. — [arXiv 2603.19771](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.19771v1)
- **L3Cube-IndicSBERT** (Deode et al., arXiv 2304.11434, Apr 2023): multilingual BERT fine-tuned SBERT-style on translated NLI/STS for 10 Indic languages; reported to outperform LaBSE, LASER and paraphrase-multilingual-mpnet-base-v2 on Indic cross-lingual and monolingual similarity. No romanised evaluation found. — [arXiv 2304.11434](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.11434)
- **bhasha-embed-v0** (MuRIL-based, 768-d, Apache-2.0, June 2024): self-described as the first embedding model supporting romanised Hindi (hin_Latn) alongside Devanagari and English; romanised eval sets created by transliterating Hindi with indic-trans; scores published only as images/CSV (not verified). — [Hugging Face: AkshitaS/bhasha-embed-v0](https://huggingface.co/AkshitaS/bhasha-embed-v0)
- Normalisation of Roman-script Hindi (search-snippet level): a noisy-channel normaliser trained on ~30,000 Hindi word pairs reportedly reached ~78% accuracy (Sharma et al., shallow parsing pipeline for Hindi-English code-mixed text); Singh, Choudhary & Shrivastava (2018) cluster spelling variants by shared context in noisy corpora; ICON 2022 work uses phonetic representations because "there is no fixed spelling standard"; the `dhvani` library maps variants like "bahut/bohot/boht" to one canonical form via IPA (vendor-reported gains). — [arXiv 1604.03136](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1604.03136); [arXiv 1804.00804](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1804.00804v1.pdf); [ICON 2022.icon-main.33](https://preview.aclanthology.org/fix_video/2022.icon-main.33.pdf); [PyPI dhvani](https://pypi.org/project/dhvani/)
- Soundex/Metaphone were designed for English; Indian-specific Soundex variants have been proposed for Hindi/Gujarati and Indian names, evaluated only on small or non-standard datasets. — [IJCSEA 2014 (PDF)](https://airccse.org/journal/ijcsea/papers/4314ijcsea03.pdf); [GJEIS 2016](https://gjeis.com/index.php/gjeis/article/view/839)
- Intake24 itself uses Metaphone 3 (English) plus Levenshtein ≤2 with minimum word lengths 3/6, in addition to synonyms. — [intake24 search-settings.ts](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/common/src/surveys/search-settings.ts)

### Inferences
- For STEADY the upstream LLM parse already acts as a strong "normaliser" (it can emit both a canonical English description and the canonical romanised dish name). The DB layer then needs to absorb residual spelling variance: an alias table (canonical row ← romanisation variants, regional names: chapati/chapatti/chapathi/phulka/roti; dahi/dhai/curd; chaas/chhaas/chaach/buttermilk) plus pg_trgm similarity.
- A deterministic "skeleton" key (lowercase → strip non-letters → collapse doubled letters → fold common romanisation pairs such as aa→a, ee→i, oo→u, th→t, dh→d, w→v, ph→f) stored as an extra column could catch many variants at near-zero cost. This is opinion/design proposal, not evidenced; it must be validated on STEADY's eval set because over-folding causes false matches (cf. Intake24's "cake"→"sage" warning).
- Dense multilingual embeddings for Hinglish should be treated as optional: the evidence (Mazumder et al. 2026) shows mainstream encoders align romanised Hinglish poorly; a model trained with romanised data (e.g., bhasha-embed or a commercial multilingual embedder) would need to be tested on STEADY's own queries before adoption.

### Gaps
- No published evaluation found of LaBSE, multilingual-e5, bge-m3 or commercial embedders on romanised Hinglish *food-name* retrieval.
- No standard benchmark for romanised Hindi spelling normalisation was found.

## Q7. Defaults for generic terms ("milk", "rice", "roti", "chicken")

### Takeaway
Every authoritative system avoids "first row wins": USDA FNDDS uses dedicated NFS/NS codes whose nutrient values are consumption-weighted composites; INFOODS prescribes a consumption-weighted mean of ≥3 plausible items or the most common form for the population; Intake24 ranks by locale-specific reporting frequency (or a curated fixed priority). For a consumer app this translates into a curated, region-specific default row (or composite) per generic term, later refined by observed selection frequency.

### Cited Findings
- FNDDS: "When a survey respondent in NHANES is unable to answer all questions about a food/beverage or if detailed questions are not asked, a food code is selected that contains the term NS (not specified) or NFS (not further specified)… Nutrient values and portion weight data for the NFS or NS food codes are based on food consumption data from WWEIA, internal data on the frequency of reports, food production and supply statistics, and food industry publications." Proportions for "11100000 Milk, NFS", "82101000 Vegetable oil, NFS" and other top-reported NFS codes are reviewed each 2-year cycle, e.g., using USDA ERS Food Availability data for the mix of fat-content milks. — [FNDDS 2019-2020 documentation](https://www.ars.usda.gov/ARSUserFiles/80400530/pdf/fndds/2019_2020_FNDDS_Doc.pdf)
- INFOODS: for unspecific foods, match ≥3 items and use a weighted mean by market share/consumption (preferred) or arithmetic mean; choose the most common cooking method and most commonly consumed form (fortified vs not) for the population; consider rural/urban (whole milk rural vs semi-skimmed urban); exclude implausible items (e.g., rice/soy milk where not considered "milk"). — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Intake24: search ordering default is "Popularity (reporting frequency)", per locale, "constantly gathered and updated with each recall submission"; alternative "Predetermined order" assigns a fixed priority per food. Food list guidance: for less common foods include "the most frequently consumed item … e.g. Gala apple"; collapse variants into one entry with a stated assumption (custard "made with powder (assuming semi-skimmed milk)"). — [intake24 surveys.json](https://github.com/intake24/intake24/blob/main/packages/i18n/src/admin/en/surveys.json); [Intake24 Localisation Toolkit](https://actionlabnet.atlassian.net/wiki/spaces/ILT/pages/1605967/Food+Lists+-+detailed+guidance)
- NDS: when nothing is defensible, return an explicit unsupported result "rather than silently substituting the nearest food"; most residual errors are choices among near-duplicate variants distinguished by attributes absent from the text. — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)

### Inferences
- STEADY should add a `generic_defaults` mapping (term × region → food_id or composite, with an INFOODS-style quality code B/B2 and a "why" note), seeded by a nutritionist for the top ~50–100 generic words (milk, rice, roti, dal, chicken, egg, bread, tea, coffee, curd, oil, sugar), and use it whenever the parsed food has no distinguishing descriptors. US rows can point directly at FNDDS NFS codes (e.g., "Milk, NFS"), which already encode US consumption weighting. India has no NFS-equivalent in INDB/IFCT, so Indian defaults must be chosen (e.g., which milk fat level, cooked vs raw rice) — the specific choices need Indian consumption data (e.g., NSSO/NIN), which was not researched here.
- Later, an Intake24-style per-region popularity counter (how often users accept/choose each row for a given query term) can replace or re-rank curated defaults, but only once volume is sufficient; until then a fixed predetermined priority is the safer analogue.

### Gaps
- No source found describing how INDB, IFCT or Indian dietary surveys define defaults for generic terms like "roti" or "milk".
- How ASA24 chooses which NFS code a respondent lands on (search ranking vs probe defaults) was not documented in sources found.

## Q8. How to evaluate matching accuracy

### Takeaway
Measure retrieval and decision separately: recall@k (is the right row in the slate?), top-1 precision/recall/F1 with abstention allowed, and an "equivalence-aware" score that credits rows with the same energy/macros; include deliberate no-match items so abstention is tested; label matches with INFOODS A/B/C quality codes; report end-to-end nutrient error (e.g., kcal or carbs within a tolerance) alongside. Human gold standards should use dual coding with adjudication.

### Cited Findings
- NDS evaluation design: 1,000 held-out meal descriptions generated from NHANES recalls with retained source FNDDS codes (3,597 reference foods); metrics: recall@5, top-1 precision, recall, F1 under "strict source identifier" and "equivalence-aware" (siblings with matching energy and macronutrients credited) scoring; separate end-to-end NutriBench evaluation with Acc@7.5 g, MAE and answer rate. — [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- Lemay et al. (J Nutr 2026) benchmark: 1,304 NHANES ingredient descriptions labelled match/no-match against 256 reference foods (693 matchable, 611 no-match) — explicitly evaluates abstention; similarity thresholds alone did not separate match from non-match. — as reported in [arXiv 2608.10363](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.10363)
- NutriBench metrics: Acc@7.5 g (share within 7.5 g carbohydrate), MAE, answer rate; reported by country and by number of items. — [arXiv 2407.12843](https://arxiv.org/html/2407.12843v6)
- Intake24's semantic-search prototype evaluates with MRR@1, MRR@10, MRR@50. — [intake24/foodSearcher](https://github.com/intake24/foodSearcher)
- INFOODS quality codes (A exact; B mean/recipe/similar; C poor; D borrowed/default) plus source + item number for every match. — [FAO/INFOODS v1.2](https://www.fao.org/fileadmin/templates/food_composition/documents/Nutrition_assessment/INFOODSGuidelinesforFoodMatching_version_1_2.pdf)
- Australian Health Survey 2011-13 coding QA: started with 100% dual coding of food and measure codes; once coders reached a 90% accuracy level, dual coding was gradually reduced to no lower than 50% of households; adjudication compared the two coders' results and fed errors back. — [ABS Australian Health Survey Users' Guide 2011-13](https://www.abs.gov.au/ausstats/abs@.nsf/lookup/1704029A0DB81477CA257CD2001CA22E)
- Participant-coded vs investigator-coded food records: median differences <±17% for energy, macronutrients and minerals, but much larger for vitamin C and beta-carotene — coder choice matters most for micronutrients. — [ASU record of the study](https://asu.elsevierpure.com/en/publications/agreement-in-participant-coded-and-investigator-coded-food-record) (search-snippet level)
- StandFood's evaluation separated classification accuracy (89%) from description accuracy (79%) on 532 unseen items — another example of scoring stages separately. — [PMC5490521](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5490521/)
- Bruch et al.: a convex-combination fusion weight can be tuned with only a few labelled examples — so even a few hundred labelled STEADY queries are enough to tune fusion. — [arXiv 2210.11934](https://arxiv.org/abs/2210.11934)
- AI-assigned GI matches for USDA codes: 75.0% initial AI accuracy but only 31.3% retained after manual curation — automated self-assessment overstates quality without human adjudication. — [GI database PDF](https://www.sochob.cl/web1/wp-content/uploads/2024/08/Development-of-a-national-database-for-dietary-glycemic-index-and-load-for-nutritional-epidemiologic-studies-in-the-United-States.pdf) (search-snippet level)

### Inferences
- A practical STEADY gold set: ~300–500 real (anonymised) parsed food strings stratified by source (Hinglish dish, English generic, US packaged, misspelled/voice-transcribed), each labelled with the acceptable food_id(s) (allowing an equivalence class), an INFOODS quality code, and "no acceptable row" where applicable; two labellers on a subset with adjudication; report recall@3/@10 of retrieval, top-1 accuracy, abstention precision, and kcal-within-±10% end-to-end. Keep it held out from any tuning (consistent with the project's existing eval-harness rules on held-out data).
- Track top-1 accuracy separately for generic terms (where the "right" answer is the curated default) and specific dishes (where it is a unique row), because the failure mechanisms differ (ranking ties vs retrieval misses).

### Gaps
- No published inter-rater agreement (kappa) specifically for human food-to-FCDB matching was found; only adjacent figures (AHS 90% coder accuracy threshold, categorisation kappas).
- No published gold-standard set for Indian dish names matched to INDB/IFCT was found.
