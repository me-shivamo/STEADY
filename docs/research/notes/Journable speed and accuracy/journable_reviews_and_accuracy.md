## App Store and Google Play ratings and review themes

### Takeaway
Journable has high headline ratings: 4.7 on Google Play from about 124K ratings and 1M+ downloads, and 4.75 to 4.86 on iOS, but iOS has only about 1.2K ratings across five big storefronts. Users mostly praise how easy it is: you type it like a text message, there is no signup or long questionnaire, and logging is quick. The biggest complaint by far is the paywall. People get about 20 free AI entries, and many feel the limit is hidden until it runs out. Second come accuracy complaints: wrong macros, wrong numbers even when they photograph a nutrition label, the same food getting different calories on different days, and portion errors. Positive accuracy comments are almost all vague ("seems accurate"). Negative ones are specific and checked against labels or a scale.

### Inferences
- Users rate the experience, not the accuracy. Five-star reviews praise ease and almost never cite a verified number. Negative accuracy reviews nearly always include a concrete check against a label, a restaurant figure or a scale. That pattern suggests accuracy problems are real but invisible to casual users.
- The overwhelmingly positive Play histogram (80% five-star) alongside a 56% paywall share among one- and two-star reviews suggests most churn comes from monetization, with accuracy second. Accuracy complaints come mostly from engaged users (macro trackers, people who weigh food), the same people a "lean-pro gainer" product like STEADY targets.
- Same-input, different-output reports fit an LLM estimating nutrition directly, without a fixed database lookup or cached per-user food memory. That is an inference from the symptoms, not something Journable has confirmed.
- The Play rating count (124K) is about 100 times the iOS count, so Journable's scale is largely on Android and in markets like India. App Store sentiment alone would understate its reach.

### Gaps
- Apple's RSS feed only exposes about 10 to 50 recent or helpful reviews per storefront, so iOS themes come from a small sample (44 reviews). A full iOS review export would need a paid tool such as AppFigures or Sensor Tower.
- Play's review endpoint returns sorted subsets. The 1-, 2- and 3-star samples were deliberately pulled, so they over-represent negatives compared with the true 80% five-star distribution. Use the "200 newest" stats for base rates.
- The exact free-entry limit and current regional pricing could not be confirmed from an official Journable page. User reports range from 5 to 20 entries and $8 to $10 (or £7.99) a month.

## Independent accuracy and speed tests, and comparisons with other AI trackers

### Takeaway
No journalist, dietitian, academic or YouTube test that measures Journable against weighed food turned up, and no head-to-head logging-time measurement either. The only third-party test is a QA bug report (QAwerk, 2025-11-27). It found the same plate photographed from two distances produced 630 against 470 kcal, a 25% swing, plus a meal-total arithmetic bug. The best independent benchmark for the category is an NIH study (NUTRITION 2026, July 2026). It found Cal AI, MyFitnessPal, Lose It! and Appediet photo logging underestimated meals by 252 to 345 kcal, about 33%. Journable was not tested, but nothing suggests a photo-plus-LLM pipeline like Journable's would do better. On store metrics Journable sits with the best-rated apps (4.70 on Play), but at roughly half of Cal AI's rating volume and a fraction of MyFitnessPal's.

### Cited Findings

#### Third-party testing of Journable itself
- QAwerk "Bug Crawl" (published 2025-11-27, iPhone 14 Pro Max, iOS 18.6.2) found 4 bugs, 2 of them rated Critical. (1) "Calorie/nutrient estimates vary significantly for identical plate photos taken at slightly different distances": the same plate came out at 630 kcal and 470 kcal. (2) "Removing zero-calorie item reduces total meal calorie count incorrectly": a 620 kcal meal dropped to 356 kcal after removing 0.001 tbsp of olive oil. Two were rated Major: (3) a lactose-free cappuccino showed more calories and macros than a regular one, and (4) the meal edit page fails to open while the calendar dropdown is expanded. The verdict was "generally accurate and easy to use" and "a very good AI-powered calorie counter", but "refining the AI calorie-counting logic would make the tool even stronger." #### Category benchmark: NIH weighed-meal test of photo AI (Journable not included)
- Researchers from NIH/NIDDK (Charles O et al., presented by Aaron Hengist, PhD) photographed 102 nutrient-controlled meals from a metabolic kitchen. They compared the apps against national food composition tables. The work was a conference abstract at NUTRITION 2026 (2026-07-25 to 28) and is not yet peer-reviewed. Mean energy underestimation (95% CI): Appediet 252 kcal (210 to 295), MyFitnessPal 327 kcal (269 to 385), Lose It! 333 kcal (282 to 383), Cal AI 345 kcal (296 to 392). That is about 33% of meal energy. Fat was underestimated by about 30 g across apps, and Lose It! and Cal AI underestimated carbohydrates by about 14 g. Hengist: "These apps tend to underestimate calories, especially from fats, so what they actually ate is likely higher than what the app shows." — [Healio, 2026-08-04](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260804/ai-photobased-calorietracking-tools-underestimate-them-by-33); [HealthDay, 2026-07-27](https://www.healthday.com/health-news/nutrition/how-accurate-are-photo-based-calorie-apps-4-are-put-to-the-test)
- Preliminary follow-up results (more than 200 additional meals) suggest even larger underestimation on high-fat, low-carb meals. — [Healio](https://www.healio.com/news/primary-care/20260804/ai-photobased-calorietracking-tools-underestimate-them-by-33); [MedicineNet/HealthDay](https://www.medicinenet.com/how_accurate_are_photo_based_calorie_apps/news.htm)

#### Vendor-run "independent" tests (conflict of interest; Journable absent)

### Inferences
- Without a database fallback, barcode scan or verified food entries (all repeatedly requested, see section 1), Journable's numbers likely come from model estimates. The NIH results show photo-AI estimates miss about a third of energy, mostly fat. Journable users' scale and label complaints (meat 2 oz logged against 5.5 oz weighed, Huel 320 against 400 kcal, McDonald's fries 75 kcal) all point in the same direction: underestimating energy-dense foods.
- Journable's speed advantage is real but measured only by users' own reports: chat-first entry, no signup, no onboarding questionnaire, "one click away". Its edge over MyFitnessPal and Lose It is friction, not proven accuracy.
- Store ratings don't separate accurate from inaccurate trackers. Journable, SnapCalorie and MacroFactor all score about 4.7 to 4.8 despite very different methods (LLM chat, photo depth estimation, verified database). Ratings mostly track ease and pricing perception.

### Gaps
- I found no independent weighed-food or lab test of Journable, so there is no measured error rate for it.
- I found no independent time-to-log or tap-count study that includes Journable. Speed evidence is limited to user reviews and vendor blogs.
- I found no YouTube review with a transcript or test of Journable in search results. YouTube itself wasn't searched directly.
- Journable's AI provider and model are unconfirmed. One user claims "It uses OpenAi", but Journable doesn't say.

## Community discussion, non-US/Indian food handling, and exploitable weaknesses

### Takeaway
Journable has almost no visible community footprint. I found no Reddit, Hacker News or YouTube discussions through search. Reddit blocked direct access: the search tool refuses reddit.com, and Reddit's public JSON API returned an HTML block page. Product Hunt shows 1 review. Its distribution looks app-store and ad driven, with a large India and Android base. Feedback on Indian and non-US food is thin and mixed. The exploitable gaps are clear and consistent: hidden paywall resentment, non-deterministic numbers for repeat foods, weak label OCR and branded or restaurant lookup, no barcode or database fallback, poor correction flow, no "same as yesterday" memory, no health-platform sync, and goals that don't adapt.

### Inferences
- Opportunities for STEADY, mapped to these gaps: (a) deterministic, cached per-user food memory, so "same as yesterday" returns identical numbers; (b) database-grounded macros (USDA, INDB, IFCT) instead of model-invented numbers, with labels read into structured values; (c) a combined photo-plus-text or voice input; (d) corrections that edit the entry in place; (e) transparent free-tier messaging; (f) health-platform sync; (g) adaptive goals. Gaps (a) and (b) line up directly with STEADY's existing macro-resolution architecture (cache, then USDA, then AI estimate), which targets exactly the inconsistency and label-accuracy complaints Journable users raise.
- Journable has a real India foothold (iOS IN rating count is close to the US count), and its Indian-food evidence is mixed and thin. A tracker that visibly handles Indian dishes and portions (katori, roti counts, dal consistency) using IFCT/INDB data has a credible differentiation story there. That is an inference: no source quantifies Journable's Indian-food error.
- Journable's strongest moat is perceived ease. Its "no signup, no 21 questions" onboarding and text-message logging earn praise, so STEADY should match that friction level before competing on accuracy.

### Gaps
- Reddit, Hacker News and YouTube sentiment couldn't be retrieved: Reddit was blocked, and no Hacker News or YouTube results appeared in search.
- No source quantifies Journable's accuracy on Indian, Asian or other non-US cuisines, or on non-English input.
- Journable's DAU or MAU, revenue and retention couldn't be found (Similarweb and AppGoblin pages exist but weren't fetched).
