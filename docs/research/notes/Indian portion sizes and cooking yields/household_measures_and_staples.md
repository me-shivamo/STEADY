# Indian household measures and portion weights of single-ingredient staples

Research date: 2026-11-13. Confidence grades used below: **A** = several measured sources agree; **B** = one good measured or official source; **C** = weak, secondary, nominal or single-blog.

A note on method: the ICMR-NIN *Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024* PDF (24 MB, 148 pages) was downloaded from nin.res.in and its text extracted locally, so the NIN numbers below are quoted from the document, not from summaries. USDA FoodData Central portions were pulled from the FDC API (SR Legacy) on 2026-11-13. Where a number comes only from a search-engine snippet and the source was not opened, it is flagged "(snippet; not opened)".

## 1. What do ICMR-NIN publications give for household measures?

### Takeaway
NIN's 2024 guidelines publish a **measured** standard utensil set: katoris of **115 ml and 155 ml (small), 200 ml (medium) and 360 ml (large)**, with **tablespoon = 15 g and teaspoon = 5 g**. Everywhere else in the document, "1 cup/katori = 200 ml". The infant charts give weighed gram-per-spoon and gram-per-katori values for several raw foods. These show that "1 teaspoon" is really 3–5 g depending on the food, not a fixed 5 g.

### Cited Findings
- **Annexure I, "Suggested measuring katori/cups and spoons"** (printed p. 111; PDF p. 128): Large Katori **C6 – 360 ml**; Medium Katori **C7 – 200 ml**; Small Katori **C8 – 155 ml**; *Small Katori **C9 – 115 ml**; **Tablespoon 15 g; Teaspoon 5 g**. — [ICMR-NIN, Dietary Guidelines for Indians 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- **"General considerations for measuring food items"** (printed p. 112; PDF p. 129) lists NIN's full standard cup set: "C1=1500 mL, C2=1100 mL, C3=900 mL, C4=700 ml, C5=470 ml, C6=360 ml, C7=200 ml, C8=155 ml, C9=115 ml, C10=95 ml, C11=70 ml, C12=25 ml". It says "Only 4 cups i.e., C6, C7, C8, C9 were used here as these are most commonly used in households." — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- Same page: "Due to substantial variances in home measures such as measuring cups, spoons, or katoris, it is difficult to collect accurate information…" and "We have employed tablespoons and teaspoons in order to standardize most of the measurements (these are similar in most households)." — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- How they measured: "Diet and kitchen scale (Seca Culina 852) was used to measure all the weights." For leafy vegetables, "only leaves are weighed. The leaves were placed neither too tight nor too loose in the bowl." Fruits were weighed as edible parts, peeled and chopped. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- On size choice: "Fruits and vegetables of small/medium size were chosen for weight estimation (banana, oranges, etc). The same for eggs as well." Also: "A difference of 5g in weight is not very apparent in the katori (eg. 155 ml & 150ml look almost similar in a 155ml katori)." — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- **Annexure II, raw-food measures in household utensils** (printed pp. 113–114; PDF pp. 130–131; infant diet charts), weighed values:
  - Rice 10 g = **2 teaspoons** (≈5 g/tsp)
  - Wheat flour 10 g = **3 teaspoons** (≈3.3 g/tsp)
  - Ragi flour 10 g = 3 teaspoons
  - Redgram dal 5 g = 1 teaspoon
  - Rajmah 5 g = 1 teaspoon = 8 beans
  - **Oil/ghee 5 g = 1 teaspoon**
  - Spinach 20 g = ¾ of a medium (200 ml) katori
  - Amaranth 20 g = 1 full medium (200 ml) katori
  - Colocasia leaves 20 g = ½ of a big (360 ml) katori
  - **Egg 20 g = ½ egg**
  - **Orange 50 g = ½ orange without peel**
  - Banana or mashed apple 40 g = ¼ of a small (115 ml) katori
  - Bottle gourd 25 g = half a small (115 ml) katori
  
  — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- The sample adult diet plans carry the footnote "**\* Measures: 1 cup/Katori = 200ml**" (e.g., PDF p. 80, and repeated with later plans). All quantities in those plans are raw ingredients. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- Elsewhere in DGI 2024: salt is limited to "5g (1 teaspoon) of common salt per day", and the advice is to "Limit ghee or butter to just 1 to 2 teaspoons/day". The text uses the 5 g teaspoon convention throughout. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- NIN's older national-survey "reference" bowl sizes, as quoted by a 2016 Haryana study, are **small 105 ml, medium 140 ml, large 200 ml** (attributed to NIN/NNMB surveys). — [Gupta et al., Indian J Community Med 2016 (PMC4919938)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)

### Inferences
- NIN's "teaspoon = 5 g" is a **label for a 5 ml measuring spoon**, not a density-independent constant. NIN's own weighings give 5 g/tsp for rice and dal but 3.3 g/tsp for flour. For ghee/oil, 5 g per level 5 ml spoon implies a density of 1.0 g/ml, which is a little high for fats (USDA's butter oil works out to ~0.87 g/ml; see §3). So NIN's 5 g is a rounded-up convenience figure.
- Two NIN size sets exist: the 2024 measured set (115/155/200/360 ml) and the older NNMB reference set (105/140/200 ml). They agree that **200 ml is the top of the "everyday katori" range** and **~115–155 ml is a "small" katori**. They disagree on what "medium" means: 200 ml in 2024 vs 140 ml in the NNMB set.
- NIN's infant charts show that a katori-to-gram conversion is meaningless without a food-density step. 20 g of loosely packed spinach fills ¾ of a 200 ml katori, but 200 ml of dal weighs ~200 g.

### Gaps
- *Nutritive Value of Indian Foods* (NIN, Gopalan et al.) household-measure tables: not located or verified in this session.
- DGI 2024 gives **no glass/tumbler volume, no ladle (karchi) volume, and no plate-serving weights**. Its spoon values are given in grams only, with no ml figure and no level vs heaped definition.
- NIN does not say how many katoris were surveyed to choose C6–C9, or from which regions. The text says only that these four sizes are "most commonly used in households".

## 2. What do Indian portion-size / utensil-calibration studies report (katori, glass, spoon)?

### Takeaway
Indian surveys mostly **pick one nominal utensil size** rather than calibrating against real homes: katori 150 ml (Kolkata FFQ), katori 200 ml and glass 200 ml (LASI-DAD national survey), katori "200 g" and glass 200 ml (North-India FFQ). The one study that **measured real household serving bowls** (rural Haryana) found them far larger, at 240/320/600 ml. No Indian study measuring household spoon volumes was found. The best South Asian measured spoon data are from Sri Lanka: household teaspoons average **4.0 ml** and household tablespoons **9.0 ml**, well below the nominal 5/15 ml.

### Cited Findings
- **LASI-DAD** (Longitudinal Aging Study in India – Harmonised Diagnostic Assessment of Dementia, Wave 2, 2022–24; 4,465 older adults; 22 states/UTs). "A standard bowl called Katori … used for curry/dal (lentil) measures **200 ml**". "A standard glass used for consuming water, tea, juice etc measures **200 ml**". "A standard table spoon and teaspoon measures **15 and 5 ml** respectively." — [LASI-DAD diet methods paper, BMC Public Health 2025 (PMC12573831)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/)
- LASI-DAD's pre-test used "glasses, Katori, and spoons of three different sizes". Interviewers found these "confusing and logistically cumbersome", so the study standardised to one size per utensil. No regional differences in utensil volume were reported. — [PMC12573831](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/)
- **Kolkata adolescent FFQ** (Rathi, Riddell, Worsley; Nutr J 2017; ages 14–16). Pictorial aids defined "katori (**1 katori = 150 ml**), glass (**1 glass = 250 ml**) … and cup (**1 cup = 200 ml**)". The authors state, "These serving sizes specified in the FFQ do not relate to any national dietary guidelines." Banana, egg and omelette were counted in pieces (1, 2, 3+), and ice cream in scoops. — [Rathi et al., Nutr J 2017 (PMC5571590)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571590/)
- **Rural Haryana utensil survey** (Palwal district, 9 villages; 27 households; **74 serving utensils measured: 58 bowls and 16 glasses/tumblers**). Derived local portion sizes were **small 240 ml, medium 320 ml, large 600 ml**, against NIN/NNMB reference sizes of 105/140/200 ml. Reference portions "would have underestimated the food consumed by the child by 55–60%" (abstract). Utensil volume was measured against a standard 240 ml cup. Weights were taken on a Seca Culina 852 scale. The per-utensil table (Table 2) is an image and was not readable, so the glass volumes are not available. — [Gupta et al., Indian J Community Med 2016 (PMC4919938)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)
- A northern-India FFQ validation used a **katori labelled as 200 g** and a **200 ml glass** as visual aids (snippet; full text not read in an earlier STEADY research pass). — [NMJI 2014;26(5)](https://nmji.in/nmji/archives/Volume-26/Issue-5/OA-II-26-5-2014.pdf)
- A North-India FFQ development paper reportedly "measured the weights of three sizes of spoons, bowls, and glasses and averaged them". The actual values could not be retrieved because ResearchGate returned 403. — [Development of an FFQ for the Northern Region of India (ResearchGate)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/311775849_Development_of_a_Food_Frequency_Questionnaire_to_Assess_Dietary_Intake_for_the_Residents_of_the_Northern_Region_of_India)
- **Measured household spoons, Sri Lanka** (Galle district; 50 households; 45 teaspoons, 15 tablespoons; filled with water, weighed, triplicate). Teaspoons averaged **4.02 ± 0.97 ml (range 2.89–7.76)** and tablespoons **9.00 ± 3.39 ml (range 4.25–15.04)**. **93.3%** of both spoon types held less than the nominal 5/15 ml. Spoons were "filled to their maximum volume" (brim). — [PLOS Global Public Health 2025 (PMC12880656)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12880656/)
- **Measured household spoons, Greece** (Falagas et al. 2010). Teaspoon capacity ranged **2.5–7.3 ml** and tablespoon capacity **6.7–13.4 ml** (snippet). — [Int J Clin Pract 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1742-1241.2010.02402.x). Wikipedia's summary of the same study says 71 teaspoons from 25 houses were surveyed. — [Wikipedia: Teaspoon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Teaspoon)

### Inferences
- An Indian user's "1 tablespoon" of ghee or oil, taken with a household serving or dessert spoon, is probably **closer to 9–10 ml than 15 ml**. The Sri Lankan data are the nearest culturally similar measurement (steel cutlery, similar table habits), but this transfer to India is an assumption.
- Survey choices of 150 ml (Kolkata) vs 200 ml (LASI-DAD, NMJI, NIN 2024) reflect what the designers considered "a katori", not measured household means. The only measured Indian sample (Haryana) is rural, child-focused and about **serving bowls** rather than eating katoris. That probably explains why it is 1.5–3× larger.

### Gaps
- No published Indian measurement of **household teaspoon/tablespoon volumes** was found. A Mumbai paediatric dosing study (caregivers measuring 5 ml of paracetamol with household spoons; 18.65% of 386 erred) appeared only in a search summary without a verifiable URL, so it is not cited.
- No utensil-calibration volumes were found for **NNMB, ICMR-INDIAB, CARRS, PURE-India or INDDEX24-India**. The only NNMB numbers available (105/140/200 ml) are second-hand, via Gupta 2016.
- **Steel tumbler** volume (common in South India, e.g., the filter-coffee tumbler/davara) and **ladle (karchi)** volume: no measured Indian data found.
- **"Plate" servings**: no measured Indian data found within this scope.
- No Indian **food photographic atlas** with published utensil weights was accessed. The Lady Irwin College photo-series tool (Mediterranean J Nutr Metab 2023) was noted in earlier STEADY research, but its tables were not accessible.

## 3. What do USDA FoodData Central and other global comparators give?

### Takeaway
USDA gives precise **edible-portion** weights by size: banana **81/101/118/136/152 g** (XS to XL); egg **38/44/50/56/63 g** (small to jumbo); butter oil (ghee) **12.8 g per tbsp**; peanuts **1.0 g each, 146 g per cup**. These are measured US reference weights and are the most reproducible numbers available.

### Cited Findings
- **Bananas, raw (FDC 173944, SR Legacy)**: extra small (<6″) **81 g**; small (6–6⅞″) **101 g**; medium (7–7⅞″) **118 g**; large (8–8⅞″) **136 g**; extra large (≥9″) **152 g**; NLEA serving 126 g; 1 cup sliced 150 g; 1 cup mashed 225 g; 89 kcal/100 g. — [USDA FDC 173944](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173944/nutrients)
- **Egg, whole, raw, fresh (FDC 171287)**: small **38 g**, medium **44 g**, large **50 g**, extra large **56 g**, jumbo **63 g**; 1 cup = 243 g (4.86 large eggs); 143 kcal/100 g. — [USDA FDC 171287](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171287/nutrients)
- **Butter oil, anhydrous (FDC 173412)**, the USDA's ghee equivalent: **1 tbsp = 12.8 g**; 1 cup = 205 g; 876 kcal/100 g. — [USDA FDC 173412](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173412/nutrients)
- **Peanuts, all types, raw (FDC 172430)**: 1 oz = 28.35 g; 1 cup = 146 g; 567 kcal/100 g. **Peanuts, dry-roasted, unsalted (FDC 173806)**: **1 peanut = 1.0 g**; 1 cup = 146 g; 587 kcal/100 g. — [USDA FDC 172430](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/172430/nutrients); [USDA FDC 173806](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173806/nutrients)
- **Health Canada, Nutrient Value of Some Common Foods (Table 7)**:
  - Egg, hard-boiled, 1 large = **50 g** (78 kcal)
  - Cooked egg white, 1 large = 33 g
  - Cooked egg yolk, 1 large = 17 g
  - Egg, fried, 2 large = **92 g** (173 kcal)
  - Eggs, scrambled, made with 2 eggs = **124 g** (189 kcal)
  - Omelet, cheese, made with 2 eggs = **150 g** (273 kcal)
  
  — [Health Canada Table 7](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/nutrient-data/table-7-eggs-egg-dishes-nutrient-value-some-common-foods-2008.html)
- **Norwegian Food Composition Table, "Omelette, fried in fat"**: 181 kcal and 14.4 g fat per 100 g; **1 portion = 125 g**; values "calculated as recipe" (EuroFIR procedure). — [Matvaretabellen](https://www.matvaretabellen.no/en/omelette-fried-in-fat/)
- **"Handful" of nuts, measured** (Brown et al. 2021; Dunedin, New Zealand; n = 120 adults; each person served three of six nut types). Median (IQR) grams, Table 2:
  - **Peanut**: handful **33.1 (16.9)**; small handful 14.6 (9.9); large handful 58.3 (20.2); "usual serving" 29.2
  - **Almond**: handful **40.8 (21.0)**; small handful 17.1 (10.5); large handful 66.3 (31.0); usual serving 27.3
  - All nuts: handful 36.3; small 16.7; large 61.3
  
  For peanuts, men's median handful was 35.7 g and women's 32.5 g. Sex was not a significant predictor of handful size. Hand size was not measured. — [Brown et al., IJERPH 2021 (PMC8345392)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8345392/)
- **Almond piece weight**: the Almond Board serving is "1 ounce (28 g) or about **23 almonds**" (≈1.2 g each; snippet). — [Almond Board, All About Almonds](https://www.almonds.org/sites/default/files/content/attachments/allaboutalmonds.pdf). USDA NASS's 2023 California Almond Objective Measurement report gives an **average kernel weight of 1.67 g** (Nonpareil 1.69 g), up 14% on 2022 (snippet; the PDF could not be downloaded from this machine). — [USDA NASS 2023 Almond OM](https://data-origin.nass.usda.gov/Statistics_by_State/California/Publications/Specialty_and_Other_Releases/Almond/Objective-Measurement/2023almondOM.pdf)
- **Ghee by volume** on converter sites: ~4.5–4.6 g per teaspoon and ~13.4–13.8 g per tablespoon. These are derived from an assumed 220 g/cup, not measured. Low confidence. — [converters-encyclopedia (ghee tsp→g)](https://converters-encyclopedia.pages.dev/cooking/teaspoons-to-grams/ghee/)

### Inferences
- USDA portion gram weights in SR Legacy describe the **edible portion**: peeled banana, egg without shell. This is consistent with Health Canada's hard-boiled large egg (50 g) and with the Indian layer handbook's "57 g egg ≈ 50 g food material" (§5). An Indian food blog that calls USDA's 118 g "unpeeled", with 85 g edible, has misread the USDA table (see §5 banana).
- USDA butter oil works out to 12.8 g / 14.79 ml ≈ **0.87 g/ml**. A level measuring teaspoon (4.93 ml) therefore holds ≈ **4.3 g** of ghee, and a level tablespoon 12.8 g.
- USDA peanuts at 146 g/cup imply ≈ **9 g per level measuring tablespoon** (146/16). This is inferred, not a USDA-listed portion.
- Energy consistency check for a 2-egg omelette: 2 × 50 g egg at 143 kcal/100 g = 143 kcal, plus ~5 g oil ≈ 44 kcal, gives ≈ 187 kcal. That nearly matches Health Canada's 2-egg scrambled (189 kcal, 124 g). The 124 g likely includes milk.

### Gaps
- **FAO/INFOODS** density or portion guidance was not reviewed in this session.
- USDA portions for **almonds per piece, omelette (FNDDS), granulated sugar per tsp/tbsp and vegetable oil per tsp/tbsp** could not be pulled: the FDC DEMO_KEY hit its rate limit (HTTP 429) and the project has no USDA key in `.env`. They should be fetched before being used as constants.

## 4. Where sources disagree (katori 100 vs 150 vs 200 ml, etc.), why?

### Takeaway
The katori disagreement is mostly **definitional**. Different sources name different points on a continuum of utensils (115 → 155 → 200 → 360 ml), and FFQs pick one point by fiat. Real household serving bowls, at least in rural North India, are larger still. Spoon disagreement comes from **measuring spoon vs cutlery spoon** and **level vs heaped**. Household cutlery runs 20–40% smaller than the nominal 5/15 ml when filled level.

### Cited Findings
- NIN itself lists **four** "commonly used" household katoris spanning 115–360 ml, and labels two different sizes (115 and 155 ml) as "small". — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- NIN/NNMB reference sizes (105/140/200 ml) vs a measured rural Haryana set (240/320/600 ml): the reference set underestimated children's intake by 55–60%. — [Gupta 2016 (PMC4919938)](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4919938/)
- FFQ designers' choices: 150 ml katori, 250 ml glass, 200 ml cup in Kolkata, with no link to national guidelines ([PMC5571590](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5571590/)); 200 ml katori, 200 ml glass, 15/5 ml spoons in LASI-DAD, after three sizes proved too confusing ([PMC12573831](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12573831/)).
- Household spoons hold less than nominal: in Sri Lanka, 93.3% were below 5/15 ml, with means of 4.0 ml and 9.0 ml ([PMC12880656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12880656/)). In Greece, teaspoons ranged 2.5–7.3 ml and tablespoons 6.7–13.4 ml ([Falagas 2010](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1742-1241.2010.02402.x)).
- Ghee per spoon varies by source: NIN uses 5 g/tsp and 15 g/tbsp as round numbers ([DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)); USDA's level measuring tablespoon is 12.8 g ([FDC 173412](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173412/nutrients)).

### Inferences
- **Why 100/115 ml**: older NNMB "small" (105 ml) and NIN C9 (115 ml) are the small steel *vati* used for dal or curd at the thali.
- **Why 150 ml**: 150–155 ml (NIN C8; Kolkata FFQ) is the common "regular" eating katori.
- **Why 200 ml**: NIN's survey-standard "cup/katori" (C7), adopted by LASI-DAD. It is also the brim capacity of many medium katoris, which are rarely filled to the brim.
- **Why 240–600 ml**: these are serving bowls, measured in rural homes and sized for refills or shared serving. They are not the eating katori.
- **Liquid vs dry fill**: liquids are served below the brim (spill margin), while dry foods (rice, poha, dry sabzi) can be mounded above it. Without measured fill data, a medium katori probably holds ~150–180 ml of dal and anywhere from ~120 g (fluffy rice) to ~200 g (mounded sabzi). This is inference; no study measured it.
- **Level vs heaped spoons**: ghee is semi-solid at Indian room temperatures, so a "spoon of ghee" scooped from a jar is often heaped and could be 1.5–2× a level spoon. No measurement was found.
- **Regional differences**: no source quantified regional katori or glass sizes. The 22-state LASI-DAD deliberately ignored them, and Haryana is the only measured region.

### Gaps
- No study measured **typical fill level** (as a percent of brim) for katoris by food type.
- No study measured **heaped vs level** grams of ghee, sugar or peanuts in Indian household spoons.
- No **region-by-region** (North/South/East/West, urban/rural) measured katori or tumbler data was found.

## 5. Staple portions and recommended defaults with S/M/L ranges

### Takeaway
Where Indian measured data exist (NIN utensils, Karnataka banana weighings), they broadly agree with USDA. Where they don't (handful, spoon fill, omelette oil), the best evidence is non-Indian measured data (NZ handful study, Sri Lanka spoons). Recommended defaults are below. Each traces to the cited findings, and the grade reflects evidence quality.

### Cited Findings

**Banana**
- **Karnataka variety weighings** (Main Horticultural Research Station, University of Horticultural Sciences, Bagalkot; two seasons, 2021–22 and 2022–23; net-house vs open field; pooled; pulp and peel weighed on a digital balance):
  - **Grand Naine: pulp 124.94 g, peel 37.23 g**. This was the highest of the varieties studied.
  - **Ney Poovan (Yelakki/elaichi type): pulp 50.49 g, peel 9.44 g**. This was the lowest.
  - Rajapuri had the thickest peel (2.56 mm).
  - Across varieties, mean pulp weight was 88.91 g (net house) vs 78.73 g (open field).
  
  — [Plant Archives: Impact of net house and open field cultivation on quality parameters of banana varieties](https://www.plantarchives.org/article/128-Impact%20of%20net%20house%20and%20open%20field%20cultivation%20on%20quality%20parameters%20of%20banana%20(Musa%20spp.)%20varieties.pdf)
- USDA (see §3): small **101 g**, medium **118 g**, large **136 g** edible. — [FDC 173944](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173944/nutrients)
- NIN 2024 chose "small/medium" bananas for its weighings. Its infant chart uses 40 g of banana as a ¼ small-katori serving, which gives no whole-fruit weight. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- A food blog (low confidence) gives Yelakki at **~40–50 g peeled** and a "medium Robusta" at 118 g unpeeled / 85 g edible, citing USDA. The 85 g contradicts USDA, where 118 g is itself the edible medium weight. — [hassanchef.com](https://www.hassanchef.com/2026/01/calories-in-1-banana-kela-khane-ke-fayde.html)

**Eggs**
- **India's Table Eggs Grading and Marking Rules (Agmark, 1968)**, individual in-shell weights: **Extra large ≥ 60 g; Large 53–59 g; Medium 45–52 g; Small 38–44 g**. Label colours are white, red, blue and yellow respectively. The weight bands come from the search summary of this document and a reproduction on indiaagronet. Text extraction of the PDF here confirmed the grade and colour table but not the weight column. One site gives medium as 46–52 g. — [Table Eggs Grading and Marking Rules (pashudhanpraharee PDF)](https://www.pashudhanpraharee.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/09/TABLE-EGGS-GRADING-AND-MARKING-RULES.pdf); [indiaagronet Agmark table](https://indiaagronet.com/indiaagronet/Technology_Upd/contents/Standard%20and%20Norms.htm)
- An Indian commercial-layer handbook gives a hen's egg as ~**57 g**, supplying ~**50 g of food** (≈18 g yolk + 33 g albumen) (snippet). — [Handbook for Commercial Layer Farming in India](https://www.pashudhanpraharee.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/A-HAND-BOOK-FOR-COMMERCIAL-LAYER-FARMING-IN-INDIA.pdf)
- NIN 2024: **½ egg = 20 g** (small/medium eggs chosen), implying ~40 g per egg. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- USDA edible: 38/44/50/56/63 g ([FDC 171287](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/171287/nutrients)). Health Canada: hard-boiled large 50 g; fried 2 large 92 g; scrambled 2 eggs 124 g; cheese omelette 2 eggs 150 g ([Health Canada](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-nutrition/healthy-eating/nutrient-data/table-7-eggs-egg-dishes-nutrient-value-some-common-foods-2008.html)). Norway: omelette portion 125 g, 14.4 g fat/100 g ([Matvaretabellen](https://www.matvaretabellen.no/en/omelette-fried-in-fat/)).

**Ghee**
- NIN: teaspoon 5 g, tablespoon 15 g; oil/ghee 5 g = 1 teaspoon. — [DGI 2024](https://nin.res.in/dietaryguidelines/pdfjs/locale/DGI_2024.pdf)
- USDA butter oil: 1 tbsp = 12.8 g. — [FDC 173412](https://fdc.nal.usda.gov/food-details/173412/nutrients)
- Household spoons in Sri Lanka hold a mean of 4.0 ml (tsp) and 9.0 ml (tbsp) at brim. — [PMC12880656](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12880656/)

**Peanuts and almonds**

Supporting notes on these tables:
- For whey, the app should prefer **brand-specific label serving sizes** whenever the user names a brand. A "scoop" is defined by each manufacturer's included scoop, and MuscleBlaze labels some variants as a *heaping* scoop.
- For eggs, India's Agmark grades are **in-shell** weights, while USDA/Health Canada weights are **edible** weights. Using the handbook's 57 g → 50 g food, shell is ≈12% (inferred), so Agmark large (53–59 g) ≈ 47–52 g edible and medium (45–52 g) ≈ 40–46 g edible.
- For bananas, "1 banana" in most Indian cities most likely means a Cavendish-type fruit. A user who says "elaichi kela" or "Yelakki" means a fruit less than half that size, so the variety name should switch the default.
- For katoris, a sensible UX is to treat **200 ml as the default capacity** and offer a one-time "calibrate your katori" (fill with water, weigh). NIN itself notes that home utensils vary substantially, and the 115–360 ml spread is about 3×. This matches the approach already in `docs/testing/portion-weighing.md`.

### Gaps
- **No Indian measured "handful"** study (peanuts or almonds). The NZ handful data assume Western-sized almonds and peanuts and adult hands.
- **No measured Indian omelette** data (raw egg weight, oil used, cooked weight). The omelette numbers are reconstructed from egg weights plus Western cooked-dish tables.
- **No measured ghee per household spoon** (level or heaped) in India.
- **Indian egg market weight distribution** (what share of eggs sold are medium vs large; desi egg weight) was not found. The Agmark bands are a grading standard, not a market survey.
- **Banana**: only one Indian measured study was opened (Karnataka, two varieties plus Rajapuri). Robusta specifically (vs Grand Naine), Rasthali, Poovan, Nendran and Red banana per-finger edible weights were not verified. Nendran figures (pulp 89.2 g, peel 49.3 g) appeared in a search snippet without a confirmable source and are not used.
- **Whey**: other Indian brands (AS-IT-IS, Avvatar, Nakpro, MyProtein India) were not checked. Brand-page values came via search summaries, not direct page reads.
- **Sugar per teaspoon** (USDA ~4 g level, unverified this session) and **oil per tbsp** USDA values need a direct FDC pull.
